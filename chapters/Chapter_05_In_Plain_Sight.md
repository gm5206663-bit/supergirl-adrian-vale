# Season 1, Episode 2 — Stronger Together

## Chapter 5 — In Plain Sight

*Earlier that week*

James brought the cape to the roof in a plain box.

Kara recognized the weave before he explained where it had come from. She ran her fingers across fabric made on a world that no longer existed, and for a moment the city below them seemed impossibly far away.

“My cousin kept this?”

“He kept the blanket he arrived in. This was made from it.” James watched her lift it carefully from the box. “He encouraged me to come here. But leaving Metropolis was my decision. I wanted a life that wasn't just standing next to his.”

“And now you're standing next to mine.”

“If you'll let me.”

She looked up at him, then down at the red cloth spread between her hands. It was a gift, not an instruction. She could accept it without deciding that being saved had made her own efforts unnecessary.

That afternoon, Alex called with a direct number for DEO operations. Henshaw had agreed to let Kara work with them. She would share her movements; they would share information that could keep her alive. Training would begin when Alex cleared her arm.

Adrian left the facility that evening after another interview. Henshaw required further appointments and contact details, but did not attempt to turn voluntary cooperation into an arrest whose consequences he could not control. The conviction remained under investigation. There was no pardon, and Adrian did not ask for one.

At home, he closed the seal himself. Distant traffic faded into the ordinary sounds of his apartment. Then he checked the fitted case he kept ready for emergencies and set it beside his work bag. Having a way to leave the seal did not mean he intended to leave his identity on the evening news.

For several days, the arrangement held.

***

The missile found Kara just as she decided she had lost it.

Its reflected flare appeared in the curve of the canyon wall. She rolled, felt the heat pass her shoulder, and climbed hard enough that the ground became a map beneath her. A second missile came up from below. This time she waited, let it commit to its turn, then dropped between two ridges and heard it detonate against empty stone.

Alex's voice sounded in her ear. “Better.”

“That sounded almost like a compliment.”

“It was. Don't make me regret it.”

Kara grinned and banked toward the observation station. Her arm no longer hurt; the torn costume had been repaired, and the new cape followed her turns without coming apart. For the first time since the power plant, flying felt like something she could enjoy rather than something she had to survive.

Then Winn called about the harbor.

She passed the location to Alex before changing course. By the time she reached the waterfront, black smoke had swallowed the roofs of two loading sheds. Flames ran along a fuel-slicked stretch of pier toward an oil tanker. Men were still coming down its gangway.

Kara landed beside a stack of pallets, drew breath and blew toward the fire.

The result was immediate and wrong. Flame flattened, then spread beneath a parked trailer. A firefighter shouted a warning as burning debris skittered across the concrete.

She stopped. “Winn, that made it worse.”

“I heard. I'm calling Adrian. Stay on.”

***

Adrian took the call at his desk. He asked for the berth number, picked up his bag and walked into an unused changing room on the guest floor. The door locked behind him. He released the band there, out of sight, and the remaining movements took scarcely longer than drawing breath.

Black fitted clothes replaced his office shirt and trousers. Boots, gloves, then the long black coat, its split hem clearing his legs and its high collar settling against his neck. He covered the band beneath the overlapping glove and sleeve before fastening the mask.

Its sculpted black edges swept up over his brows and down across his nose and upper cheeks. The case's mirror showed the same arrangement he had checked before: no loose strap, exposed cuff or piece of ordinary work clothing. He closed the case on those clothes and left it secured in the room.

He checked the service exit before moving through it. Once beyond the sheltered loading area, he climbed clear of the building and turned toward the harbor.

***

The tanker had to move. Kara flew to its side, planted her hands beneath the curve of the hull and began to lift. Steel groaned above her fingers. She stopped at the sound, but the plate had already started to dimple.

“Not there.”

A man in black was beside her, one gloved hand against the hull, not lifting it. The long coat moved around his legs in the wind. An angular mask covered his upper face; the raised collar and long coat gave him a silhouette unlike the colleague she saw at work.

Winn had told her who was coming. Kara kept his name to herself.

“The ship can float,” he said, his voice low beneath the noise of the fire. “Let it.”

On deck, a crewman was pointing toward the bow. Kara followed his gesture and saw the lines holding the vessel against the quay. Beyond the smoke, a tug was trying to reach the far side.

She flew up to him. “What do you need?”

This time she waited for the answer.

The crewman shouted instructions into a radio, then pointed her toward a heavy mooring line. Kara carried it clear while Adrian went to the bow. With the remaining berth lines released and the tug taking the stern, they began drawing the tanker away from the burning pier. Kara could feel the difference through the rope: steady resistance instead of metal yielding beneath her palms.

A section of gangway collapsed behind them.

Adrian was gone from the bow before the people on it reached the water. He caught one man under each arm, crossed the gap and set them beyond the fire crews. Then he returned for a third who had fallen against the rail.

Kara kept the line taut. The tug's horn sounded; the crewman gave her a broad sweep of his arm. She changed direction with him, and the tanker turned slowly into open water.

By the time the last line came free, foam was covering the burning slick along the pier. The ship had a damaged plate and frightened crew, but its cargo remained inside it.

Kara landed beside him while an ambulance attendant examined the men he had carried out. Across the cordon, a cameraman turned his lens toward them. Adrian kept the mask on. When the attendant asked whether anyone else remained on the broken gangway, he answered with a shake of his head and pointed toward the crewman who had supervised the evacuation.

There was nothing on him that belonged to Cat's office: no office shirt, badge, watch or visible band. To the people filming, he was the man in black who had come to help Supergirl.

Kara waited until they were beyond the microphones before speaking.

“Thank you.”

He inclined his head. “Get the crewman's name. You listened to him. Let them hear that too.”

Then he left over the water, still masked, instead of flying straight back to his workplace. Kara stayed with the people whose ship they had moved.

***

By the afternoon meeting, Cat had three usable views of the rescue and no confirmed name for the man in black.

She played the clearest angle twice. The mask, high collar and moving coat gave the newsroom an unmistakable figure to print, but not an employee to identify. Beside Kara, Adrian turned a page in the ordinary shirt he had retrieved after returning unseen through the service entrance. His work bag was under his desk, the case closed inside it. He had re-engaged the seal before joining the others.

“Two flying people,” Cat said. “And neither of them has made an appointment with a journalist. It's almost rude.”

A producer suggested asking the harbor crews whether the man had given them a name.

“Do that. And use the word unidentified until you have an answer. A black coat is not a biography.” Cat changed the image to Kara's failed attempt to blow out the fire.

Maxwell Lord appeared in the next clip, condemning untrained intervention around hazardous cargo. The tanker had not spilled, but the first burst of flame made an excellent loop for television.

Kara looked down at her notes. Adrian did not try to make her feel better by pretending the footage showed something else.

Cat let the clip finish. “Now that we've established how easily a rescue becomes a headline about liability, perhaps we can ask Supergirl what she's actually trying to do.”

James shifted in his chair. Cat noticed.

“An interview, James. With her. Not a statement from her cousin and not three men arguing about whether she should have lifted the ship.”

Adrian passed the harbor office's contact details to the producer beside him. Cat took them when they reached the end of the table and went straight on to the next item.

For the rest of the meeting, Cat needed him to arrange people, rooms and times. He finished the notes and sent them to the people waiting for them.

***

The stinger lay in a clear evidence tray beside a dead guard's identification card.

Kara had been called to Plastino Chemicals the next morning. Alex led her past the broken security door and showed her the puncture in the guard's uniform before uncovering the recovered projectile. Its color pulled a memory out of Kara with startling force: her mother arriving home late, putting down her case, answering a child's question about the prisoner she had sentenced that day.

“A Hellgrammite,” Kara said.

Henshaw looked up. “You're certain?”

“I remember my mother describing one. The stingers. The way his mouth changed.” She studied the torn cabinet behind Alex. “Did he take chemicals?”

“Several containers. We're comparing this with other thefts.”

On the drive back, Alex let her examine the photographs instead of telling her to leave the investigation to professionals. Kara found she could be grateful for that and still hate the sight of the guard's card. He had been smiling when someone took the picture.

At the DEO, Alex took her to the training room.

“There are low-level kryptonite emitters in here,” she said before opening the door. “They're off. If you agree, we'll use them to put us close enough physically that I can teach you without your accidentally throwing me through the wall.”

Kara looked through the glass. “You weren't going to tell me that last week, were you?”

Alex did not try to smile it away. “I'm telling you now.”

Kara considered the room, then nodded.

Knowing the weakness was coming did not make it pleasant. Her knees felt less certain beneath her. When Alex told her to attack, she lunged too directly and found herself on the mat with her sister holding her wrist.

“Again,” Alex said, helping her up.

The second time, Kara tried harder. That only gave Alex more momentum to use. The third time she stopped watching her sister's hands and noticed the shift of weight that came before the throw. She still went down, but she understood part of what had happened.

Sweating and furious, she sat on the edge of the mat.

“Someone stronger can still walk through all of this.”

“Yes,” Alex said. “But that isn't a reason to give the person in front of you every opening you can.”

Kara looked at the faint mark where the axe had cut her. It was nearly gone.

“Show me the turn again.”

***

Astra watched the harbor footage with her arms folded.

Her subordinate stopped the recording as the black-coated man crossed the gap above the water. There was no crest she recognized. His face was masked, his wrists covered, and the camera had caught no useful conversation between him and Kara.

“Another Kryptonian?” the soldier asked.

“Possibly.”

“Do you know him?”

“No.” Astra studied the image, then gestured for him to continue. Guessing at a face behind a mask would not tell her what had happened to Vartox. “Find out whether he stays with the girl.”

They still had no answer from the missing operative. Astra had ordered the places his handler routinely used cleared while they established what the humans knew. The warehouse where she would receive a prisoner was a separate rendezvous, not a place she intended to keep her people.

The Hellgrammite arrived there under guard. He glanced at the empty loading bays before looking at Astra.

“I need food. Not a war.”

“Then stop drawing attention to yourself every time you take it.”

“Your soldiers found me.”

“So will the humans. When they do, you will bring me one of their agents. Alive.”

He looked at the guards. Neither moved aside.

Astra turned the screen toward him. Kara was visible above the harbor, the man in black landing nearby.

“If either of these two is with them, don't try to fight both. Watch where they take the shipment. Learn their route. There will be another opportunity.”

The Hellgrammite studied the masked figure without recognizing him. Then his gaze returned to the red cape.

“She put us in prison.”

“Her mother did,” Astra said. “Remember the difference.”

She let the guards escort him out. The humans had taken something from the power plant. Before she sent more soldiers against them, she wanted to know what weapons they possessed and whether Vartox had spoken.

***

“Is Supergirl supposed to do nothing until she can do everything perfectly?” Kara asked.

Cat looked up from the proposed front page. “Is that what I said?”

“No, but—”

“Then don't improve my argument into something easier to object to.” She tapped the harbor photograph. “She saw a fire and tried to put it out. Admirable. She made it worse. Less admirable. Then she listened to someone who knew how to move a ship, and things improved. There's a lesson in there that doesn't require her to retire.”

Kara glanced at the second photograph, the masked figure above the pier.

“What if people decide they don't need her?”

“People needed three different emergency services, a tugboat and two flying people yesterday. I doubt we're in danger of having too much useful help.” Cat handed back the page. “Tell James I still expect an answer.”

Kara found him near the elevators. A few minutes later, Winn joined them in the alley behind the building and stopped when he saw who was already there.

“You know?” he asked James.

James looked from him to Kara. “You know?”

“Yes,” Kara said. “And now we can stop having every conversation twice.”

Winn glanced upward, checking the windows. “That would save a lot of time.”

She wanted their help finding things she could do well, and learning the things she could not yet do well. Not a campaign that turned every rescue into a test of whether the city liked her. James knew people who worked on the ground. Winn could find calls that needed a faster response than the city could provide.

That evening, James directed her to a stranded ambulance before its patient ran out of time. Winn found a frightened child whose pet was stuck in a tree. He neglected to establish that Fluffy was a snake until Kara already had it draped over both arms.

By the time she returned it, she was laughing.

Later, carrying groceries up six flights for an elderly woman whose elevator had failed, Kara realized she had not thought about the harbor for nearly an hour. The woman offered her an orange. Kara accepted it and ate it on the roof while James listened to her account of the snake.

When the laughter faded, he told her about Cat's latest demand.

“I came here because I wanted to do work people could call mine,” he said. “Apparently I brought my most marketable friendship with me.”

“You don't want to ask him.”

“I don't want you to think you owe me an interview because someone can make my job unpleasant.”

Kara turned the orange peel between her fingers. “Cat already has that passenger recording. What if looking at me across a table is all she needs?”

James looked toward the lit windows of CatCo. “Then I can't promise she won't see you.”

It was not comforting, but she trusted it more than reassurance he could not support.

“I still want people to hear from me,” she said. “Let me work out how.”

***

Alex found the link in the laboratory results: chlorine-based biology, with the stolen pesticides serving as food rather than the components of a bomb.

By the following afternoon, a map of the thefts covered the briefing-room screen. The DEO could move a shipment through a route the Hellgrammite had already used, but Henshaw no longer treated the expected arrival of one escaped prisoner as proof there would be only one attacker.

“Vartox had orders,” he said. “We still haven't identified who gave them. Assume someone may be watching this.”

Kara stood beside Alex instead of outside a closed door. Adrian was across the table, attending one of the appointments he had agreed to keep. He had returned to the seal after the harbor. Henshaw had asked whether he would assist if they drew the prisoner out; the black case stood beside Adrian's chair.

Adrian studied the proposed route.

“That crosses two open slip roads.”

“We can close them,” Alex said.

“And the truck?” Kara asked. “If he attacks it, someone should be there who can keep the chemicals off the road.”

Henshaw adjusted the assignments. The truck would cross an isolated service stretch under surveillance, with the approaches closed to traffic. Alex would travel in the escort vehicle; Kara would cover the cargo and crews. Adrian would stop the attacker before he could reach the crews or escape. He would change and release his seal before deployment, not wait for someone to be hurt. Field reports would use the assigned channel; neither his civilian name nor his prison identity needed to travel over an open radio.

It was less tidy than Henshaw's first diagram. It also gave them an answer to more than one thing going wrong.

As the others collected equipment, Kara stayed beside the map.

“Are you going to keep helping?” she asked Adrian.

“When I agree to something, I'll do it.”

“That isn't quite what I asked.”

“No.” He looked through the glass at the agents checking their gear. “I'm not looking for a new uniform. I still wanted the job I went to yesterday morning.”

“I know.”

“She made you sit through the whole editorial meeting,” Kara said.

“Someone still had to take down the changes.”

“You gave them the harbor office's number.”

“The crew did their jobs. They deserve to say what happened.”

Kara smiled a little. “Cat asked you to help James get the interview, didn't she?”

“I told her he was already handling it.”

“You didn't offer to ask me?”

“That's your answer to give.”

Outside, Alex raised a hand: time to go.

Kara moved toward the door, then looked back. “Thank you for that.”

Adrian nodded, picked up the case and went to change in the private preparation room.

***

The first stinger struck the escort's windscreen rather than the cargo truck.

Alex ducked behind the reinforced dashboard as a second projectile punched through the side window. It grazed her upper arm. She ordered the driver to brake and dropped out behind the door, keeping the vehicle between herself and the direction of the shots.

The Hellgrammite landed on the roof. His hand reached toward the shattered passenger window.

It never reached the metal.

A black-gloved hand caught his wrist. Adrian lifted him clear of the vehicle and set him on the road with his arm held behind him, coat settling around his boots. The entire movement was over before Alex could bring her weapon around the door. She kept it lowered when she saw who had him.

Beyond them, the cargo trailer swerved. Another stinger from the opening attack had punctured a tire. Kara reached the truck before its rear wheels crossed the edge of the road, bracing against the chassis where the load was carried rather than pushing on the thin side of the tank. The driver kept the brakes engaged while she brought it to a stop.

The prisoner strained once against Adrian's grip. Nothing moved except his own shoulder. He twisted his head and saw the mask from the harbor recording.

He had not seen either flyer before committing to the attack. The food had been close enough to smell, the escort apparently human. Now the space between himself and the truck might as well have been the wall of a cell.

Alex approached with restraints. Adrian kept the mask on while the prisoner was secured and moved inside the closed command vehicle. There were no reporters or civilian crew members at the operation; beyond the perimeter, the road remained closed.

“Let her finish securing that,” Adrian said when the prisoner jerked away from Alex. Here, away from the public microphones, he used his ordinary voice.

The Hellgrammite stopped. He stared at the uncovered line of his jaw, then up at the eyes behind the mask.

“That voice.” His breathing changed. “Kael-Varr?”

Adrian looked at him. “Yes.”

The prisoner's shoulders sank. He no longer tried to pull against the restraints.

“I didn't know.”

Kara came in after the truck driver confirmed the cargo was secure. She heard the name, saw the change in the prisoner's face, and stayed clear while Henshaw took Alex's place at the restraint station. Adrian stepped back without uncovering his face or offering the prisoner another name.

“What were you going to do with our agent?” Henshaw asked.

“Take her to Astra. I was supposed to bring one of you.”

Kara went still.

“Where?” Henshaw asked.

The prisoner gave a warehouse address, then repeated it while an agent marked it on the map. It was where Astra had told him to bring a captive, he said. She would be waiting there, not at the place she actually lived.

“I need to eat,” he added. “I can't live on your food.”

“We'll establish what you need,” Henshaw said. “That isn't a bargain for testimony.”

Adrian listened to the address again. Over the protected team channel, he confirmed it with Henshaw, then stepped outside. The prisoner had supplied a lead, not a reason to leave Alex or Kara walking into it alone.

He was above the road before the first vehicle started its engine.

***

Two guards stood inside the warehouse's loading entrance.

Adrian reached the doors from above, listening as he approached. He heard voices inside, one of them familiar, and no immediate cry for help. He reported the occupied building over the team channel before stepping through the entrance.

Both guards brought their weapons up.

He took the nearer barrel out of line with the street, pulled the weapon free and caught the second man's wrist before he could fire. The exchange lasted less than a breath. Adrian placed both weapons on a stack of crates behind him and let go of the wrist.

“Don't reach for them.”

A woman emerged from the room beyond the loading bay.

“What happened?” She stopped when she saw the mask. “You're the man from the harbor.”

“Astra.”

Something in his voice caught her attention. She studied him, uncertain, while the guards retreated toward her.

Adrian closed the entrance door. With the street screened from view and only his former fellow prisoners facing him, he removed the mask.

Astra's face changed.

“Kael.”

Behind her, one guard took a step back so sharply that he struck a crate. The other lowered the hand he had been moving toward a second weapon. Recognition had done what disarmament had not: made them afraid to try again.

Adrian kept the mask in his hand. None of them needed to be told what he had once done. He gave them no name other than the one they already knew.

“I thought you had left all of this,” Astra said.

“I tried.”

“Then why come here?”

“The man you sent to take an agent gave us this address.”

Her eyes moved toward the far door.

Adrian saw the decision before she completed it. When she moved, he crossed the space between them and stopped her at the exit. Her wrist met his hand; her shoulder met the closed door, held there without a blow.

The guards did not move to help her.

“Let go,” she said.

“Will you stay?”

For a moment, she pulled against him. His grip did not change. Then she stopped.

“Yes.”

He released her and stood between her and the door. Astra straightened her sleeve with a precision that did not quite conceal the effort it took to steady her hand.

Over the earpiece, Kara asked what he had found.

“Your aunt is here,” he told her. “Alive. Three people in the building. No one is hurt.”

Astra looked at him sharply. “You brought Kara?”

“She made her own decision.”

When Kara arrived, Adrian had put the mask back on and the three prisoners were standing away from the weapons. Alex and the first agents were still on the road. He let Kara enter without giving Astra room to reach her unopposed.

Kara stopped just inside the door.

Knowing her aunt might have survived had not prepared her for the movement of her face. Nor had hearing Adrian confirm it made the sight ordinary. This was not the fixed image of her mother that Alex had brought to the apartment. Astra turned toward her. She breathed. She said her name.

“Kara.”

“You're really here.”

For an instant, Astra's expression softened. Then she looked toward the masked man by the far door, and Kara understood how much of the stillness in the room was fear.

“I wanted to find you,” Astra said.

“You could have spoken to me without sending someone to take Alex.”

“Alex?”

“The agent your man tried to grab.” Kara stopped herself from explaining more. “He killed someone at the chemical plant. Then he came to work for you.”

“I didn't send him to kill a guard.”

“But you sent him to take a prisoner.”

Astra did not deny it. “I needed to know what these people had done with Vartox.”

“He's alive. Was bringing down the plane your order?”

Outside, vehicles approached. Astra listened to them, then looked back at Kara.

“I will not answer questions about my people in front of armed humans.”

“You were going to make one of them answer yours.”

The comparison tightened Astra's mouth. “There is more at stake than one agent. I watched our world destroy itself while people who could have stopped it refused to act. I will not let that happen here.”

Kara remembered the dead guard's identification card. She remembered the harbor crew trying to tell her where to put her hands.

“If saving people means you don't have to listen to them, how do they tell you to stop?”

“You sound like your mother.”

“I miss her,” Kara said. “I missed you too. That doesn't make this all right.”

The loading door opened. Alex came in with Henshaw, her sleeve cut above the fresh dressing. Astra looked from her to the other agents and took a step toward Kara.

Adrian moved with her. She stopped before he had to touch her again.

“You know what their prisons will be,” she said to him.

“I know what you asked your man to do.”

“I asked him to bring someone to me.”

“And now you're being asked to go with them.”

Henshaw ordered the weapons secured. Astra watched the guards comply, then looked back at Kara.

“Will you hear me without him deciding whether I'm allowed to speak?”

Kara glanced at Adrian. He had not answered any of her aunt's questions for her.

“I'll hear you,” she said. “That isn't a promise to agree.”

Astra studied her for a long moment. Then she held out her hands to the approaching agent.

Adrian remained masked throughout the transfer. When the prisoners were brought into the vehicles, the agents kept them separate and kept the doors closed. No one on the street was given a view of the man behind the black mask.

***

At the DEO, the transfer took longer than the confrontation.

Henshaw cleared the holding corridor, brought the existing kryptonite suppression system online in the designated rooms and kept Kara outside its field. Adrian stayed with her beyond the transfer vestibule while the agents completed the handover.

Through the observation glass, Kara watched Astra's strength leave her. Her aunt reached for the edge of a table and stared toward the emitters. This was not a weapon she had expected the humans to possess.

“What is that?” Kara heard her ask.

Henshaw answered from the control station. “Kryptonite.”

He did not pretend the word settled everything. The cells would remain monitored, the captured weapons isolated and the prisoners separated while his people established what they could verify.

Astra was alive. So were her guards, the Hellgrammite and Vartox. The questions had not ended because the fighting had stopped.

Kara turned away from the glass. Alex was beside her, hurt only by the shallow graze in her arm instead of whatever might have happened if the Hellgrammite had carried her away.

“She's going to tell me my mother was wrong,” Kara said.

“Then listen to what she can actually support,” Alex replied. “You don't have to decide before she speaks.”

Adrian returned from the private preparation room in ordinary clothes, his case closed and the band concealed beneath his cuff. He had re-engaged the seal before coming back into the corridor.

“She was afraid of you,” Kara told him.

“She has reason to be.”

“She still tried to leave.”

“Yes.”

Kara looked toward the holding room. “You could have hurt her.”

“I didn't need to.”

She took a slow breath. The same man who had told her most of the people he killed were innocent had just brought her aunt in alive. She could be relieved by that without pretending it erased the rest.

“Thank you.”

He nodded. “I'll see you tomorrow.”

“At work?”

“There's a revised guest schedule waiting for us.”

This time her smile came more easily. Ordinary things were still there, even if neither of them could quite return to being ordinary with each other.

After Adrian left, Alex took Kara down a quieter corridor.

The room at its end contained a projector and space enough to stand before it. When the light rose, Kara nearly said the same thing she had said in her apartment.

Then the image of Alura turned toward her.

“This one can answer,” Alex said softly. “It's an interactive program from the technology in your pod. Not the recording I brought you.”

Kara stepped forward. The image waited.

There were a hundred questions she wanted to ask, and one request she knew the light could not grant. She held her hands together until the urge to reach for her mother passed.

“Tell me about Astra,” she said. “Not only why you sentenced her. I want to understand what happened before that.”

Outside, Alex found Henshaw watching through the observation window.

“Thank you,” she said.

He nodded without turning it into an explanation. After Alex went back to her sister, he remained alone in the corridor for a moment. In the dark glass, his reflected eyes shone red.

Then he turned away.

***

Cat was in her car when James called.

“If you are about to tell me that Supergirl values her privacy,” she said, “I would like you to consider how little that sentence improves my evening.”

“She agreed to meet you.”

“Where?”

The car rose smoothly from the ground.

Cat went silent. Outside her window, the city dropped away; beneath the chassis, a red cape streamed in the air. When the car settled again, it was in a quiet place beyond the crowded streets, with enough light for Cat to see the woman who stepped around to her door.

Kara waited while Cat got out. She could have sent another message through James. She could have let the mystery of the masked man occupy the news for another evening while she put this off. Neither choice would have made the things she wanted to say less necessary.

Cat looked her over with an attention she never wasted on an unimportant story.

For an instant, Kara wondered whether the first word out of her mouth would be her name.

Instead, Cat held up her phone.

“May I record this?”

“Yes.”

“And will you be introducing me to the man in black?”

“His name isn't mine to give,” Kara said. “You asked to speak to me.”

Cat's thumb hovered over the screen. “Then let's hear from you.”

Kara stood a little straighter in the cape her cousin had sent her. She knew the others were out there: her sister, James, Winn, Adrian. Knowing she could call them did not make this their decision.

It made it easier to make her own.
