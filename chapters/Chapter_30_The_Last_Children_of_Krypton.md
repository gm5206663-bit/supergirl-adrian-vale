# Chapter 30: The Last Children of Krypton

*Episode: S02E02 - The Last Children of Krypton*

The fire started in the port before anyone on the radio knew what it was.

Kara got the call from Winn while she was still pulling on her boots. A tanker at the docks, crude oil, flames crawling up the side of the hull like it had always been there. She didn't wait for the full brief. She was already moving.

Clark was there when she arrived.

He stood on the pier in the red-and-blue, the wind pushing his cape back, watching the flames lick the sky. He didn't turn around when she landed beside him. He didn't need to.

"Same as always," he said.

"Same as always," she said.

They moved together without talking about it. She froze the worst of the fire with her breath, and the heat backed off enough that he could get close to the hull.

He grabbed the bow of the tanker. Pushed it away from the dock, toward the open water where the current would carry it away from the city.

She caught the stern and kept it steady. She matched his pace, absorbing the weight the way she had learned to.

Not brute force. Momentum. Counter-rotation. Letting the water do most of the work.

The tanker drifted into the bay. The fire burned itself out on the surface.

Nobody died. Nobody was supposed to.

Clark wiped soot from his forearm and looked at the city behind them. The lights were already coming back on along the waterfront. People were going about their evening. They didn't know what had just happened. They didn't need to.

"You ever think about it?" he asked.

"About what?"

"About coming to Metropolis." He kept looking at the city, not at her. "We do this. We do it well. We're the only two of our kind on this planet. It'd be —"

"Easy," she said.

He looked at her then. "I didn't say easy."

"I know."

She didn't say no. She didn't say yes. She just stood there on the pier, the damp air cooling the heat in her chest, and let the question sit between them the way the water sat between the dock and the bay.

A bank robbery came next. Three men, masks, guns, a vault they'd barely scratched open before Kara dropped in front of them. She didn't hurt anyone. She didn't need to. She picked up the men, one at a time, and set them on the floor with their hands behind their heads. By the time the police arrived, she was gone.

Clark was already on the roof of the bank when she landed.

"You're fast," he said.

"You're not slow."

He smiled. It was the kind of smile that didn't happen often enough. "There was a kid watching. He asked his mom if we were together."

"What did she say?"

"She said we were the good guys."

Kara nodded. That was enough.

They walked the roof together. The city stretched out around them, the lights of National City mixing with the reflected glow of the bay. For a few minutes, it was just the two of them, the quiet between the work, the thing that felt like family.

Then the radio crackled. Winn's voice, tight. "Kara, you need to come back. Now."

The tone in his voice killed the quiet.

"What's wrong?" Clark asked.

"Someone jumped off a building," Winn said. "You — you were supposed to be there. You were supposed to catch him."

Kara's stomach dropped. She hadn't been assigned to anything like that. But she'd been near the waterfront. Maybe someone had called her, not Clark. Maybe the call had gone to both of them, and she'd missed it.

She didn't waste time on the why. She grabbed Clark's arm. "Come on."

They flew. Not the casual, easy flight of the port. This was urgent. The kind of flight that cuts through the air without caring about the sound, the kind that gets you there before the ground has a chance to do its work.

The building was a mid-rise, an office block that had gone quiet at the end of the day. A crowd had gathered on the sidewalk. The police had the perimeter. Kara landed on the roof and ran for the edge.

A man stood on the ledge.

No. Not a man.

He turned around and Kara felt the world tilt.

John Corben. The assassin from the Venture launch. The man Lena Luthor had shot. The man she'd been told was dead.

He was alive. And he was wearing something that wasn't just clothing. A chest piece, green, pulsing, fused to the bone. The light of it strobed under the fabric like a heartbeat made visible.

"Supergirl," he said. He didn't sound surprised. He sounded like he'd been waiting.

Kryptonite shot from his chest in a searing green beam.

Kara dove sideways. The beam caught her shoulder, and the pain was immediate and total — like fire poured directly into her veins. She hit the roof, skidding, and the world went white at the edges. Her strength drained away. Not gone. Weakened, dragged down by something that was actively poisoning her.

Clark was there. He grabbed her, pulled her behind the cover of the rooftop HVAC unit, and the green beam struck the metal where her head had been.

"Kryptonite," he said. His voice was tight, controlled, the way it got when things were bad. "That's Kryptonite."

"I can't —" She tried to push up. Her arms trembled. The strength wasn't there. The green light had taken it.

Clark didn't hesitate. He picked her up, one arm under her knees, one behind her back, and carried her off the roof. The flight was steady, controlled, the kind of careful that told her he was carrying something fragile. She was limp in his arms. The city blurred past beneath them.

She closed her eyes. She didn't want to watch the ground.

The DEO infirmary was cold. The lights were too bright. Kara lay on the bed with her shoulder throbbing, and Clark stood at the door talking to J'onn in a voice that was too low for her to make out the words.

She could hear the tone though. Accusation. The kind of anger that came from fear.

A few minutes later, J'onn came in. His face was set. The Martian mask of calm was on, but it was thinner than usual.

"How bad?" Kara asked.

"The kryptonite is draining you," he said. "We can treat it. We will."

"What's happening with Clark?"

J'onn was quiet for a beat. "He wants to know where the kryptonite came from. He wants to know how someone got a piece of it, and how they built a weapon out of it."

"And?"

"And I told him the truth." J'onn's jaw tightened. "A shipment was stolen four months ago. From the DEO. We still haven't been able to determine who took it or how."

Kara pushed herself up on her elbows. The movement cost her. The room spun. "Four months."

"Four months."

"And Clark knows."

"Clark knows."

The silence in the infirmary was heavy. Kara could hear her own heartbeat. It was slow, sluggish, fighting through the green poison.

"He's right to be angry," she said.

"He's right to be angry," J'onn said. "But he's also wrong about what to do with it. He wants the kryptonite destroyed. Every piece. Every shipment. He thinks it's the only way."

"Isn't it?"

"It's the only way to make us vulnerable."

Kara looked at the ceiling. The lights were too bright. "What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to talk to him." J'onn turned toward the door. "And I'm going to ask him to trust me the way I've been asking him to trust me for a long time."

He left. The door closed. Kara lay there with the green light still pulsing faintly in the memory of her shoulder, and thought about the man on the ledge.

Corben had been shot by Lena Luthor. She'd seen it happen. And then he'd been revived by someone else. By something. The chest piece, the green — it wasn't just a weapon. It was a body.

Someone had turned a man into a walking piece of kryptonite.

She closed her eyes.

The next morning, Kara woke to the sound of low voices outside the infirmary door. She couldn't make out the words, but she could hear the tension in them. She sat up slowly. The green poison was fading — her strength was coming back in tentative waves — but her shoulder still ached.

She swung her legs over the bed and stood.

The corridor was quiet. The DEO always felt quiet in the mornings, before the shift started.

She walked past the bullpen. Adrian was already at his desk, the chipped mug on the corner of his monitor, the theater mask magnet beside it. The paper was open in front of him — the pattern, the names, the times, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code. He was reading through it, not looking up, working the leak the way he'd been working it for three weeks.

He didn't look up when she passed. He didn't need to. He'd know she was there.

"You shouldn't be up," he said, without turning.

"I'm okay."

"Your shoulder's still draining you. J'onn said to keep you horizontal for another day."

"I'm not horizontal."

He finally looked up. His eyes were the kind of tired that came from sleeping in a chair next to a console for three weeks, not from one night. "I'm not arguing with you about this. Not today. There's too much going on."

"I'm not arguing either. I'm just saying I'm okay."

He held her gaze for a second. Then he nodded, once. "The pattern's changed."

That stopped her. She walked to his desk and looked over his shoulder at the paper.

The names were the same. The times were the same. The parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code — all the same. But there was a new column on the right, in Adrian's hand, and it was short.

"What's that?"

"The stolen shipment," he said. "I matched the theft window against the ghost code log. The code was used three times in that window. All three times, the figure was seen at the back gate within ten minutes."

"McGill."

"I think so." Adrian tapped the paper. "The pattern holds. The leak is inside the DEO. And the shipment went out through the same window."

"Four months," Kara said.

"Four months."

She looked at the paper. At the names, the times, the pattern. At the work Adrian had been holding, the thing that couldn't be changed because it was on paper. She'd been carrying the same paper. She'd folded it and put it in her drawer the night the device had been released into the black.

"I have it," she said. "The paper. It's at CatCo."

"I know."

"You want me to bring it?"

"No. Winn's working the digital copy. We have what we need here." He closed the file on the screen and turned to face her fully. "But there's something else. Alex came in early. She's in the briefing room with the McGill file. She's going to go see him."

"She shouldn't go alone."

"She said she can handle it."

"She always says that."

Adrian's mouth twitched. Not a smile. Something closer to frustration. "I know. But she's an agent. She's been training for this. And J'onn's in the field with Clark. I'm the only one here who can sit on the console and keep the pattern running if something goes sideways."

Kara knew he was right. She didn't like it, but she knew. "Call me if it goes sideways."

"I will."

She left him at the console, the paper open, the chipped mug and theater mask magnet on the desk, the work still running. The butterfly items weren't props. They were the texture of his life.

The things he carried because they were real. Because Evelyn had left the magnet. Because Winn had given the mug. Because the paper was the thing that had kept him going when everything else was gone.

Alex was in the briefing room, and Winn was with her. They were bent over a monitor, and the screen showed a face Kara didn't recognize.

"Kara." Alex looked up. Her expression was careful, the way it got when she was balancing bad news and worse news. "You shouldn't be up."

"I'm fine." Kara pulled out a chair and sat. "Who's that?"

"Agent McGill," Alex said. "DEO. He's been on my list for a while. I think he's the mole. The one who's been feeding information to Project Cadmus."

"You have proof?"

"I have patterns." Alex tapped the screen. "Financial records, movement logs, a phone call that went to a number we can't trace but we can trace the timing. He's been in contact with Cadmus for months."

"Months," Kara said. The word tasted like the kryptonite in her shoulder.

"Four months," Winn said quietly. "Same time as the shipment."

The room went quiet.

"I'm going to go see him," Alex said. "I'm going to confront him directly."

"You want backup?" Kara asked.

"I want you in the infirmary." Alex's voice was firm. Not harsh. Just firm. "You're still recovering. I can handle one agent."

"You can handle one agent," Winn muttered.

"I said I can handle it."

Kara looked from one to the other. She could see the argument that was coming, and she could see that Alex had already decided. She knew that look. It was the same look Alex got before she went into something dangerous, and it was the look that meant the decision was made.

"Fine," Kara said. "But you call me the second something goes wrong."

"I will."

Adrian appeared in the doorway. He'd been listening from the corridor, and his face was set. "Alex. If you go, I want a check-in every fifteen minutes. Not when it's done. Every fifteen minutes."

"I'm not a kid," Alex said.

"I know you're not a kid. I'm saying it because McGill is a DEO agent, and DEO agents know the protocol. He'll expect you to follow it. He'll use it against you."

Alex stared at him. Then she nodded. "Fifteen minutes."

"Good." Adrian turned to Winn. "Keep the ghost code log running. If anything spikes, you call me before you call anyone else."

"Got it."

Alex stood. She checked the weapon at her hip, the way she always did before a field operation. Then she was gone, out the door, moving with the purpose of someone who had been training for this moment for a long time.

Winn stared at the screen after she left. "She's going to get herself killed."

"She's not," Kara said.

"You don't know that."

"I know Alex."

"And I know the pattern," Adrian said. His voice was quiet, but it was there. "If McGill is what we think he is, he's been preparing for this for months. He's had time to set things up. Alex is walking into something he's had time to build."

Kara looked at him. "You think she's walking into a trap."

"I think she's walking into a situation she can't fully predict." Adrian's eyes went to the paper on his desk. The names, the times, the parking lot, the back gate. "And I can't predict it either. Not from here. That's why I'm here. To catch it when it happens."

Kara sat in the briefing room for a long time after Winn left. The green poison in her shoulder was a dull, persistent throb.

She thought about the man on the ledge. She thought about four months.

She thought about the pattern Adrian had been holding. The paper, the names, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code. All of it, written in his hand, because the leak couldn't change paper.

And now the leak was inside the DEO. Not a ghost in the code. A person. An agent she'd probably passed in the hallway a hundred times.

She closed her eyes. The paper was in her desk drawer at CatCo. Folded. Put away the night the device had been released into the black.

She hadn't needed to look at it since. Maybe she did now.

But first, she needed to talk to Cat.

Cat's office was on the floor above Kara's. Kara took the stairs, not the elevator, because the elevator felt too slow and her shoulder hurt too much to wait.

The door to Cat's office was open. Cat wasn't at her desk. She was standing by the window, looking out at the city, a cup of coffee in her hand.

"You're up," Cat said, without turning.

"I'm up."

"Should you be?"

"Probably not."

Cat turned. She looked at Kara for a long moment, then set the coffee down on the desk. "Sit. Before you fall."

Kara sat. The chair was comfortable. She hadn't realized how much she needed to sit.

"I'm taking a leave of absence," Cat said.

Kara looked up at her. "What?"

"A leave of absence." Cat's voice was calm, measured, the way it got when she was delivering something she'd already decided. "I've plateaued here. I've done what I can do at CatCo. It's time to try something else."

"What kind of something?"

"I don't know." Cat's eyes went to the window, to the city she'd built the company to cover. "That's the point. I need to dive."

Kara blinked. "You're quoting yourself."

"I'm." Cat's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. "I've been saying that to you for a year. It's about time I lived it."

Kara didn't know what to say. The words were in her throat, but they weren't forming properly. Cat leaving wasn't something she'd planned for. Cat was the constant. Cat was the office, the coffee, the sharp edge that had shaped Kara into the person she was now.

"James is going to take over," Cat said. "As acting head of CatCo. He's ready for it."

Kara thought about James. About the way he'd looked at her on the roof. About the breakup, and the way it had been amicable and painful in the same breath. About the fact that he was going to be her boss now, and that was going to be complicated in a way that was different from before.

"You're sure?" Kara asked.

"I'm sure." Cat picked up her coffee again. "I'll come back one day. To the company, maybe not to this office. But I'll come back."

"When?"

"When the dive is done."

Kara nodded. It wasn't much, but it was something.

"You know what you're doing?" Kara asked.

"No." Cat's voice was dry, and there was a flash of the old sharpness in it. "That's the exciting part."

Kara stood. Her shoulder ached, but it was manageable now. "I'll miss you."

"I know." Cat looked at her over the rim of the coffee cup. "That's the point of having been your boss. You get to miss me."

Kara left the office with the door open behind her. The corridor felt different without the weight of Cat's presence behind it.

James was at his desk when Kara came back to the bullpen. He had a stack of papers in front of him, and he was working through them with the focused expression he got when he was figuring out a problem.

"Cat told you," he said, without looking up.

"She did."

"I didn't ask for it."

"I know."

He looked up. "I didn't want it. Not like this. Not because she's leaving."

"I know that too."

He set the pen down. "I'm going to need to be your boss now. That's going to be —"

"Awkward?"

"Awkward," he said. "And honest. I'm going to be honest with you, Kara. That's the only way this works."

She nodded. "Okay."

"Okay."

He picked the pen back up and went back to the papers. Kara stood there for a moment, then turned and walked away.

Adrian was at his desk when she came back to the bullpen. The paper was still open. The ghost code log was running on the monitor. He was working, but he looked up when she approached.

"Something?" he asked.

"Cat's leaving."

He nodded. He'd probably already heard. "I figured. The pattern doesn't change, but the city does. People move. Things shift."

"You sound like you're okay with it."

"I'm not okay with it. I'm okay with the fact that it's happening." He tapped the paper. "The pattern holds. The work continues. Cat leaving doesn't change the leak. It doesn't change Cadmus. It doesn't change what's coming."

Kara pulled out the chair across from him and sat. "What's coming?"

"A war." He didn't look away from the paper. "Graves made that clear. They're not going to stop. They built a man into a weapon. They stole a shipment. They have a mole inside the DEO. They're declaring war on every alien on this planet."

"And we're ready?"

"We're here." He looked up at her. "That's what we can be. Ready enough. The pattern holds. The paper doesn't change. We catch it when it happens."

She looked at the paper. At the names, the times, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code. At the work he'd been holding for three weeks, the thing that had kept him going when the device had taken everything.

"You've been holding this alone," she said.

"Not alone. You've been holding it too. Alex. Winn. J'onn. The team."

"But you're at the console every night."

"Somebody has to be."

Kara didn't argue. She knew that was true. She knew that Adrian was the one who sat at the desk, the chipped mug and theater mask magnet on the corner, the paper open, the ghost code log running, every night for three weeks. The ordinary man who had chosen the ordinary and was holding it because the device had tried to take it.

"What happens after this?" she asked. "After the war starts?"

Adrian was quiet for a moment. He looked at the paper, then at her. "I don't know. But I know the pattern holds. And I know that when the next thing happens, I'll be at the console. And the paper will still be here. And the names will still be the same. And we'll catch it."

Kara's first day as a reporter, not an assistant, started the next morning.

She wore a jacket she'd worn before, one that was clean and professional and didn't look like she'd been up all night fighting kryptonite-powered cyborgs. She brought her notebook. She brought the article she'd been working on, the one about the port fire, polished and ready.

Snapper Carr was in the editor's office. He didn't look up when she came in. He was reading something on his screen, and he kept reading it for a long time before he finally gestured at the chair across from him.

"Danvers," he said.

"Yes."

"You're the new reporter."

"Yes."

He looked at her then. His eyes were sharp, measuring, not unkind but not warm. "You have an article?"

"I do."

She handed it to him. He took it, read it, set it on the desk. He didn't say anything for a while.

"You have a lot to learn," he said.

Kara didn't argue. "I know."

"The port fire isn't a story. It's a press release. What's the story is who was there and why, and you haven't figured that out yet."

"I'm figuring it out."

"Figure it out faster." He tapped the article. "Come back tomorrow. With something that tells me what the city is actually thinking."

"I will."

She stood. She was at the door when he spoke again.

"Danvers."

She turned.

"I'm not Cat Grant," he said. "I'm not going to hold your hand through this. But I'll tell you when you're wrong. That's more than most editors do."

"I can work with that."

"Good."

She left the office and walked back to her desk. The bullpen was quiet. Adrian was at his desk with the paper open, the ghost code log running. The chipped mug was on the corner of his monitor. The theater mask magnet was beside it. The cartoon bandage was in her pocket. The things that had been the texture of her life for a year, still there, still real, even as everything else was changing.

She sat at her own desk and opened her notebook.

The story wasn't the port fire. He was right about that. The story was what had been happening underneath it — the stolen shipment, the agent, the pattern, the four months that nobody had been watching. She had the paper in her drawer. She had the names. She had the times.

She started writing.

By afternoon, the call she'd been waiting for came through. Winn's voice was tight, urgent.

"Kara. Alex. She's at the warehouse on Fifth. She found McGill. It went bad."

Kara was already standing. "I'm on my way."

"J'onn's in the field. He's closest. He's going in first."

"I'm closer. I'll be there in two minutes."

"Adrian's on the console. He's monitoring the ghost code. He says the pattern spiked."

"Tell him to keep watching."

She didn't wait for an argument. She dropped the pen, left the notebook, and ran.

The warehouse was an old shipping facility on the edge of the industrial district. Kara landed on the roof and dropped through a skylight into the interior. The space was open, a wide floor of cracked concrete, the smell of rust and old oil.

Alex was in the middle of it. McGill was on the ground, his hands behind his head, and Alex was standing over him with her gun drawn.

"You don't get to walk away from this," Alex was saying. Her voice was steady, but Kara could hear the strain in it. "Four months, McGill. Four months of stealing, and feeding information, and building something that hurts people."

"I didn't build anything," McGill said.

"You let them."

A door opened at the far end of the warehouse. Three people came through, and one of them was a woman Kara had never seen before. Late middle-aged, composed, wearing a practical jacket that looked like it had been chosen for function, not fashion.

Dr. Mercy Graves. The face from the Cadmus files.

"Alex Danvers," Graves said. Her voice was calm, reasonable, like she was addressing a colleague. "I was hoping you'd see reason."

"I see it," Alex said. "I don't agree with it."

"You could." Graves took another step forward. "You have skills. You have knowledge. The DEO is a resource, and they're using it wrong. They're protecting aliens. We're trying to protect humans."

"By turning a man into a walking piece of kryptonite."

"By doing what needs to be done." Graves's voice didn't waver. "Aliens are a threat. They're not like us. They don't think like us. They don't feel like us. And one day, one of them is going to decide that we're the threat. We're not waiting for that day."

"You're making the day happen."

Graves's expression didn't change. "Then you're on the wrong side."

She nodded. The three people behind her raised their guns.

Alex didn't drop hers.

The gunfire started.

Kara moved without thinking.

She was off the skylight ledge, across the warehouse floor, absorbing the momentum of the first few shots. The bullets hitting the concrete, the sparks, the sound.

She grabbed the first shooter by the arm. Spun. Used the body as a shield. Moved to the second.

The fight was fast. Physical. The kind of thing she could do without thinking because she'd done it a hundred times.

J'onn came through the far door. He moved with the quiet, deliberate precision of a Martian who had been doing this for three centuries.

The third shooter went down. Graves was alone.

Kara moved toward her, but Graves was already moving — a smoke canister erupted on the floor, and the warehouse filled with white.

Kara lunged for her, but the smoke was thick, and when it cleared, Graves was gone.

Alex was on the ground, a bullet graze on her arm, bleeding but alive. J'onn was at her side, checking the wound with the efficiency of someone who had done it many times.

Kara dropped to her knees beside them. Her shoulder throbbed. The exertion had pushed the kryptonite poison back up, and the green light was pulsing in the corners of her vision again.

"I'm okay," Alex said, before Kara could ask.

"You're bleeding."

"I'm okay."

J'onn looked up. "She's right. The graze is shallow. But we need to move. Cadmus knows we're here now."

"They knew before," Kara said. "They've known for four months."

J'onn's face was grim. "And now they know we know."

They got Alex up. Kara supported her on one side, J'onn on the other, and they moved through the smoke toward the door.

Back at the DEO, Adrian was at the console with the ghost code log open. The pattern Kara had been holding for three weeks. The same paper, the same names, the same times, the same parking lot and back gate and Marcus. He looked up when they came in, and the tiredness in his eyes was still there, but it was sharper now, focused.

"Graves got away," he said, without looking back at the screen.

"I saw," Kara said.

"The pattern spiked three times during the fight. Cadmus made moves while we were in the warehouse. They're coordinating. They're not just reacting."

"What kind of moves?" J'onn asked.

"Movement at the back gate. Two figures. Same window as the original shipment theft." Adrian tapped the paper. "They're re-stocking. Or they're moving something new in."

"Prometheum," Kara said. The word tasted like the green in her shoulder. "The thing Kelex found. The metal they used for the chest piece."

"That's what I'd guess." Adrian looked at her. "We need the Fortress scan. Clark and you need to go there and find out exactly what they're using."

"I'm not in any shape to fly to the Fortress," Kara said.

"You don't have to. Clark can go. J'onn can go with him." Adrian's voice was steady, practical, the voice of someone who had been making hard calls for three weeks and was still making them. "You stay here. You recover. You keep the pattern running with me. Winn's on the digital side. We hold the line from this room."

Kara wanted to argue. She knew she could fly. She knew the green poison was fading. But she also knew that Adrian was right — someone needed to hold the console, and the pattern, and the work. And he'd been doing it alone for three weeks.

"Okay," she said.

Adrian nodded. "J'onn. Clark's waiting at the Fortress channel."

J'onn went to the radio. Kara sat in the chair beside Adrian's desk, the paper open between them, the names and times and parking lot and back gate and ghost code all there, the work still running.

Adrian was at the console. The paper open. The ghost code log running. The chipped mug on the corner of the monitor. The theater mask magnet beside it. The things he carried because they were the texture of his life, because they were real, because the ordinary was something he had chosen and was holding.

They sat in silence for a while. Then Adrian spoke, without looking up from the paper.

"You going to stay in National City?"

The question surprised her. She thought about Clark. About Metropolis. About the roof, and the pier, and the easy thing it would be to go with him.

She thought about the pattern on the paper. The parking lot. The back gate. The ghost code. Marcus. The leak inside the DEO. The war Cadmus had declared. The city that needed her here. The man at the console who had been holding the work for three weeks.

"I'm staying," she said.

Adrian nodded. "Good."

They sat there. The ghost code log ran. The paper sat open. The city outside the DEO went on with its evening, unaware of the war that had just been declared on it.

The Fortress of Solitude was cold and silent. White walls, white floors, the crystalline structure that felt more like a cathedral than a base. Clark walked through the corridors with the familiarity of someone who had grown up here, and J'onn followed, his steps quiet on the crystal floor.

Kelex met them at the scanner. The AI's holographic face was calm, efficient, as it ran the scan on Clark's hand. The residue from the fight — the kryptonite, the green dust, the thing that had been shot at Kara — was still on his skin.

"Analysis complete," Kelex said. "The residue contains an element not present in Kryptonite naturally. Prometheum. The strongest metal known to man. It's what Cadmus used to create the chest piece. It's what allows the kryptonite to be focused into a weapon."

Clark looked at his hand. The green residue was already fading, washing away under the sterile lights. "They built it. They manufactured it."

"They did," J'onn said. "And they have more."

"I want the kryptonite destroyed," Clark said. "Every piece. Every shipment. Every scrap. If it exists, it can be used against us. It can be used against her."

J'onn was quiet. He looked at Clark for a long moment. "I have a stockpile at the DEO. I keep it because it's the only thing that could stop a Kryptonian who turns. I keep it because the DEO's job is to be prepared for every threat, and that means having the tools to face the worst-case scenario."

"And the worst-case scenario is you and me turning on the world," Clark said.

"And the worst-case scenario is someone stealing it and turning it into a weapon that hurts us," J'onn said. "Both are possible. Both are real. The question is which risk you're willing to take."

Clark didn't answer. He looked at his hand again, at the fading residue of something that had been built to kill him.

"I'm not asking you to destroy it blindly," J'onn said. "I'm asking you to trust that I know what I'm doing. That I've been doing this for a long time, and that the DEO exists for a reason."

Clark closed his eyes. He was quiet for a long time.

Then he nodded. "I'll take it. I'll find a place where it can't be found. I'll be responsible for it."

J'onn didn't look relieved. He looked like a man who had just made a decision that would be judged later, and he would bear the weight of that judgment alone.

"Alright," he said.

Clark went to the DEO stockpile. He took every piece of kryptonite the DEO had collected over the years. He packed it carefully, sealed it, and flew it to a location only he knew. A place deep, hidden, where no one would find it. He carried it there himself.

When he came back, he texted Kara a single line.

*It's done. I miss you already.*

Kara read it on her phone in the DEO bullpen. The chipped mug was on the desk beside her. The theater mask magnet was on the corner of the monitor. The cartoon bandage was in her pocket. Adrian was at his console, the paper open, the ghost code log still running. She held the phone in her hand and read the message three times.

She didn't reply immediately. She didn't know what to say. The work was done. The kryptonite was gone. But the war wasn't. Cadmus had declared it. They had declared it on all of them.

She put the phone in her pocket and looked at Adrian.

He was reading the paper. The names. The times. The parking lot. The back gate. The ghost code. The work.

"Clark's gone," she said.

"I know."

"Back to Metropolis."

"I know."

"And the war is on."

Adrian looked up. "The war was on the moment they stole the shipment. We just didn't know it yet."

"And now we know."

"Now we know." He tapped the paper. "And the pattern holds. And we're here. And we'll catch it when it happens."

She looked at the paper. At the names, the times, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code. At the work Adrian had been holding, the thing that had kept him going when the device had taken everything, the thing that was still running, still alive, because the leak couldn't change paper.

"You're not going to stop," she said.

"No." He looked at her. His eyes were tired. But they were clear. "I'm not stopping because when the device was running, and the city was gone, and every mind was taken — the ordinary was gone. The life I chose was gone. Not the band on my wrist. Not the desk. The thing the band and the desk existed for. The free mind in a free city. That was gone. And the only thing left was this paper. The only thing left was the pattern the device couldn't reach. I held it because it was the only thing still alive."

Kara didn't say anything. She just sat there, at the desk across from him. The paper open between them. The ghost code log running. The chipped mug and theater mask magnet on the corner of the monitor. The cartoon bandage in her pocket.

The things that had been the texture of her life for a year. Still there. Still real. Still alive.

Because Adrian was there. At the console. Holding the work.

She needed to check on something.

She walked to the holding area. The room was at the back of the DEO, past the lab, past the cells. It was quiet. The lights were low.

The pod man was in the bed.

He'd been there since the premiere. Unconscious. The Kryptonian from the pod, the stranger who had come out of the same sky she had come out of. He hadn't woken up. He hadn't opened his eyes. He'd just been there, breathing, alone.

Adrian was already there. He stood at the end of the bed, his arms crossed, looking at the man in the bed. He didn't turn when Kara came in.

"You don't have to do this alone," she said.

"I'm not doing it alone. I'm standing here."

"That's not the same thing."

He looked at her. "It's the same thing when there's no one else to do it."

Kara walked to the side of the bed and sat in the chair. She pulled it close.

Adrian stayed standing. He looked at the man in the bed, then at the paper in his hand — he'd brought it, the pattern, the names, the times, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code. He held it the way he held it every night, the way he'd held it when the device was running, the way he held it because it was the only thing still alive.

"You're not alone," Kara said to the man in the bed. Her voice was quiet in the still room. "I know what it's like. To arrive somewhere new. To not know anyone. To not know where you are or what's happening."

The man didn't move. His chest rose and fell, steady, rhythmic.

"I was nine when I got here," Kara said. "I had a ship and a name and a sister, and then I had nothing but a basement and a secret. It took me a long time to find people. It took me longer to trust them. But I did. And you will too. I'll make sure of it."

She reached out and put her hand on his arm. The contact was light, warm. The way you'd touch someone who needed to know they were seen.

"You're not alone," she said again.

The man's eyes opened.

They were dark. Kryptonian. The same as hers. The same as Clark's. But there was something in them that was different — something wild, something confused, something that had been hurt and was now furious.

His hand shot up. He grabbed Kara's throat.

The grip was strong. Not Kryptonian-strong, but strong enough. Kara's breath caught. She grabbed his wrist, tried to pull, but his fingers tightened, cutting off her air.

She looked at his face. The fury. The fear. The disorientation of waking up in a place you don't recognize. In a body that feels wrong. With a hand on your arm and a voice telling you you're not alone, when everything in you is screaming that you are.

He didn't let go.

The alarms started blaring. The door burst open. Adrian was there — he'd moved, fast, across the room — and J'onn and Winn were behind him, and the next few seconds were a blur of motion.

Adrian's hands on the man's arm. The pressure releasing. Kara gasping for air. Winn's voice shouting something over the alarm.

The man's eyes locked on Kara's. He didn't look away.

Then his grip slackened. He let go, and Kara fell back against the chair, coughing, her hands to her throat.

The man sat up. He looked around the room — the DEO, the holding cell, the people he didn't know, the woman he'd just tried to kill. He looked at his hands. Then he looked at Kara again.

"Where am I?" he said. His voice was rough, unused to speaking.

Kara caught her breath. "National City," she said. "You're safe."

He didn't look like he believed her. He looked like someone who had been told that before and had learned not to.

Adrian was at the door, his hand on the frame, the paper still in his hand, the pattern still open, the ghost code log still running in the console down the hall. He looked at the man in the bed, then at Kara. His expression was the one it always got when things went sideways — focused, steady, already processing.

The war had just gotten a new player. And he didn't know which side he was on yet.

But Adrian was there. At the console. At the bed. Holding the work. Holding the pattern. Holding the ordinary.

And the paper was still open. And the names were still the same. And they would catch it when it happened.
