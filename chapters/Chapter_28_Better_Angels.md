# Chapter 28: Better Angels

*Episode: S01E20 - Better Angels*

Alex had the Kryptonite suit on. The sword. The blank face. She was hovering above the city coming at Kara like a version of her sister that had been emptied out, and the thing behind the eyes wasn't Alex at all.

Kara didn't move. She didn't raise her hands. She didn't block. She just stood there and said the name.

"Alex."

The blade came. Kara didn't flinch. She said the name again, because it'd the only thing she had left to say.

The sword stopped.

Not because Alex stopped it. Because two other voices reached her first.

Eliza had been reading the logs Kara had left on the desk.

The guard log.

The ghost code log.

The paper with the times and the names and the pattern. Three weeks of it, written in Kara's hand.

The parking lot. The back gate. Marcus. The ghost code. Every time the figure had shown up. Every time the code had been used.

Eliza had read it the way she read everything, carefully, patiently, the way a woman reads when she's spent her life watching a daughter from a distance and every detail matters.

And she'd found the frequency. She'd found the symbol. She'd found the thing Kara had broadcast. And she'd done what Kara couldn't — she'd spoken into the signal, not a speech, not a plan, just the thing a mother says when her daughter is falling.

"Alex."

The word went through the signal like a hand through water. It reached the mind wearing Alex, and it reached the person underneath.

J'onn was there too.

Bleeding, leaning on the wall, the Kryptonite still in his system, but he was there.

He'd been fighting for three hundred years.

He'd seen minds taken before.

He knew you couldn't pull a mind back with logic.

You pulled it back with the thing it trusted.

He stepped forward and said the name, the low Martian sound that had been the voice of her partner for months, the voice of the man who'd been protecting her and the people she loved.

"Alex."

Two voices. Same name. Same signal. The thing wearing Alex didn't know which voice to hold on to. That was the thing that broke it.

Alex's eyes shifted. The blank cracked. The sword lowered. She looked at Kara, then at the woman behind her, and she was Alex, then she wasn't, then she was again, and the rhythm was still running but it wasn't winning.

She fell.

Kara caught her. They landed on the roof, Kara holding her sister in her arms, the Kryptonite suit cold against her ribs, Alex shaking.

"I'm sorry," Alex said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

"I know," Kara said. "I know."

***

The city didn't wake all at once.

The broadcast went out again — same frequency, same signal, the House of El symbol riding the waves.

And the people on the street stopped.

One.

Then two.

Then a dozen.

Then a whole street, then a block, then a neighborhood.

They looked around.

Looked at the person next to them.

Looked at their hands.

Looked at their phones.

They didn't know how long they'd been gone.

They didn't know what had taken them.

But they knew they were back.

The DEO was the last place to wake. The guards at the gate blinked. Looked at their posts. The tech room came back. The console came back. The ghost code log was open on the screen. The pattern Kara had been holding for three weeks.

Adrian was there behind it, reading it. The same paper Kara had been carrying. The same names. The same times. The same parking lot and back gate and Marcus.

He looked up when the lights came on.

"The city is waking," he said.

Eliza was in the doorway. She'd been at the DEO since the broadcast, and she'd been the one who'd found the frequency and the symbol and the thing that had reached Alex.

"J'onn is hurt," she said. "The blade. Indigo. He's in the medical room."

Adrian stood up. Left the console. Went to the medical room.

***

Non had moved to Fort Rozz the moment the broadcast started.

The fortress was old. Kryptonian. Built into a hillside, a prison that had become a base. The cells were open, the prisoners gone, and the thing that had been running the rhythm was still running. Because Non had a new plan.

"Myriad can't be stopped," Indigo said. She was standing beside him on the balcony, the wind in her hair, the calm of a woman who'd been working from the inside for a long time and had nothing left to hide. "The signal is in the city. The minds are taken. They'll sleep through the broadcast. They'll wake and then they'll sleep again. And you'll have the city."

"I don't want the city," Non said.

"You want the device," Indigo said. "You want the power. Your cousin is a Kryptonian. She'll come here. She'll bring the broadcast. She'll try to stop you. And she'll fail. Because the signal can't be stopped. And when it's running, the minds are mine. All of them. The city, the people, your sister, your friends. All of them."

Non looked down at the city. The people had stopped walking. The rhythm was breaking. The broadcast was reaching them — not all of them, not enough of them, but some.

"They're waking," he said.

"They'll sleep," Indigo said. "Because I'm going to do what you couldn't. I'm going to end the argument. I'm going to end the doubt. I'm going to end the hope. And when I do, the device will run, and the people will be mine, and the city will be ours."

She stepped off the balcony. She didn't fall. She moved. She went to the device.

Non followed.

The plan was already there, in the data, in the design, in the thing Indigo had been building toward.

Not the people.

Not the city.

The mind.

The thing that had been split — the Kryptonian conscience, the doubt, the argument, Astra and Non, love and hate, two halves of a soul that had been one soul.

Indigo was going to merge them back.

Every mind in the city, a single mind.

And the ones who couldn't be merged — the Kryptonians, the humans who had fought, the ones who had held the pattern on paper for three weeks — those would be removed.

The countdown was four hours.

***

Max had been tracking the signal since the moment it went out. He had the ion blocker, the satellites, the numbers. And he'd been watching the signal move — shifting, consolidating, settling.

"It's at Fort Rozz," he said. He was in the DEO tech room, the console humming, the logs open. "Non and Indigo are there. The device is there. And the signal is still running."

Kara was beside him. Alex was beside her, the Kryptonite suit off, her hands still shaking. Eliza was behind her with a hand on her shoulder.

"How long?" Kara said.

"Four hours," Max said. "They're running a countdown. They're building the thing that will make the signal permanent. The device won't stop. It won't fade. It will run. And the city will be theirs. And there will be no way back."

"I can stop it," Kara said.

"You can't stop it with hope," Max said. "You broadcast. Some of them wake. Then they sleep again. The device is still there. It's still running. You have to stop the device."

"I know," Kara said.

She looked at the room. Winn, back to himself, sitting at his desk with his hands still and his eyes clear. James behind him, the rhythm gone from his face, the man she knew back. The receptionist at the front desk, not moving in the pattern, looking at her phone like it'd new.

She looked at Adrian at the console, the paper open beside the keyboard. The names, the times, the pattern, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code. She looked at Eliza, her hand on Alex's shoulder. She looked at Alex — her sister, the one who'd been taken and had come back.

"I'm going to Fort Rozz," she said.

"I'm coming with you," Alex said.

"You just got back," Kara said.

"I'm coming with you," Alex said. "Because the next time the signal takes me, I want to be standing next to you when it does."

***

Kara went to Winn first.

He was at his desk, the paper with the pattern open in front of him, the ghost code log on the screen. He looked up when she walked in, and he looked at her the way he always did — the way someone looks at the person they've been working beside for a year when they still haven't said what they feel.

"I need to go," she said. "And I'm not sure I'm coming back. The pattern you've been holding — the paper, the times, the names, the parking lot, the back gate — it matters. It mattered because it'd the only thing holding when the leak could change everything else. And it matters now because it's the work. And I need you to keep doing it if I don't come back."

Winn looked at her. Looked at the paper. Looked at her face.

"You're coming back," he said.

"I don't know that," she said.

"You're coming back," he said. "Because you're Kara Danvers. And you don't leave. And I've been holding the pattern for you for three weeks, and I'm not stopping now."

She put her hand on his desk, on the chipped mug — the one she'd kept from him, the one with the small chip. She didn't say anything. She just touched it.

Then she went to Cat.

Cat was in her office, earrings on, the ion blocker still working, the rhythm gone. She was looking at the city through the window — the streets, the people waking, the broadcast still going out.

"I'm going to Fort Rozz," Kara said.

"I know," Cat said.

"I'm not sure I'm coming back," Kara said.

"You're coming back," Cat said. "Because I've been watching you for a year. I've seen you walk into rooms you shouldn't have gone into. I've seen you hold things you shouldn't have been able to hold. I've seen you do the work no one saw. And I'm not watching you walk into a fortress and not come back. You're coming back. Because that's what you do."

Kara looked at her. Looked at the earrings — the gift from Max, the thing that had kept her safe.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me," Cat said. "Come back."

She went to James.

He was at his desk in the bullpen, the rhythm gone, the man back. He looked up. He stood. He walked to her.

"I'm going to Fort Rozz," she said. "And I'm not sure I'm coming back."

He didn't say anything. He put his arms around her and held her. The thing between them was the same thing it had always been — not the secret, not the hero. The woman. The person. The thing he'd been trying to say for a year and hadn't been able to.

"Come back," he said.

"I'll try," she said.

She went to J'onn.

He was in the medical room. Eliza was there. The blade had missed the heart. The Kryptonite was in his system, but he was strong, and Martian biology was resilient, and he was going to live.

"You're not going alone," he said. His voice was low. The voice of a man who'd been fighting for three hundred years and wasn't done.

"I'm not going alone," Kara said. "Alex is coming with me."

"I'm coming with you," J'onn said.

"You can't fly a ship," Kara said.

"I can fight," he said. "And Indigo is there. And Indigo stabbed me. And I'm not letting her finish what she started."

Kara looked at him. Looked at Eliza, her hand still on Alex's shoulder. Looked at the paper on Adrian's desk, the pattern still open, the ghost code log still running.

"I'm going to Fort Rozz," she said. "And I'm going to stop the device. And I'm going to come back. And I need you to hold the city while I do."

J'onn nodded. Eliza nodded. Adrian looked up from the console and nodded.

***

Lucy had been at the DEO for three weeks, same as everyone else. She'd been reading the logs, same as Eliza, same as Adrian. She'd been holding the pattern. Watching the back gate. And when the broadcast went out and the city woke, she'd gone to her father.

General Lane had been at the barricades since the moment the military sealed the city. He'd been standing in the middle of the quarantine, the man with the troops and the authority and the belief that control was the answer. He'd watched the people wake. Watched the rhythm break. Watched the broadcast go out. And he'd decided it'd a threat.

"It's a mind-control device," he said. "The same device that took the city. And now it's being broadcast again. And you're standing there telling me to let it run."

"It's not the same device," Lucy said. "It's the counter. It's the House of El. It's the thing that wakes people up. And you're standing there telling me to stop it. You put the barricades up. You're holding the city. And you're calling the wake a threat because you can't control it."

"I'm protecting the city," General Lane said.

"You're protecting control," Lucy said. "And control is what took the city in the first place. You're doing the thing that let them in. You're holding the barricades and calling the wake a threat. And I'm telling you to let it in. Because the people inside are waking. And the thing that woke them isn't the thing that took them. And if you don't let them in, you're the one who will keep them out."

General Lane looked at his daughter. Looked at the barricades. Looked at the people behind them — the ones waking, the ones coming back, the ones who had been lost.

"Open the barricades," he said.

Lucy went to the gates. She stood in front of them. She looked at the people behind the barriers, then at the ones in front, and she put her hands on the metal and pushed.

The barricades opened.

***

The flight to Fort Rozz took an hour.

Kara was in the pod — the one that had brought her to Earth, the one she'd used to get to the fortress. Alex was beside her. J'onn was in the back, face set, wound bound, Kryptonite still in his system, the Martian determination that had kept him alive for three hundred years.

Kara looked out the window. The city fell away. The ocean came. The land came. The fortress appeared on the horizon — the old Kryptonian structure built into the hillside, the prison that had been a base, the thing that had been holding the signal.

She thought about the paper in her pocket.

The names, the times, the pattern.

The parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code, the leak, Marcus.

She'd held all of it because the leak couldn't change paper.

She'd held it because she didn't know who the leak was.

She'd held it because it'd the work.

And now the leak wasn't the problem.

The problem was a device that couldn't be stopped, a signal that couldn't be interrupted, a countdown that was running, and a woman named Indigo who had stabbed a Martian and had her own plans.

She didn't have a plan. She didn't have a bomb. She had hope. She had her sister. She had a Martian who was bleeding and stubborn. And she had a city that was waking — not all of it, not enough of it, but some — and that was what she was carrying. Not the pattern, not the paper, not the names. The choice.

The pod landed.

***

Fort Rozz was old. Kryptonian. Built to hold things and keep things out. And now it'd holding the device and the people who had run it.

Kara walked through the gates. Alex beside her. J'onn behind them.

The corridor was dark. The lights flickered. The signal was running through the walls, through the air, through the Kryptonite in the stone, and Kara could feel it — a pressure, a hum, a thing trying to close around her.

She walked to the device room.

Non was there. Indigo was there. The device was there — the machine built to force a mind to think as one, the machine running the city, the machine that couldn't be stopped once it'd running.

Indigo turned. She saw Kara. She didn't look surprised. She looked the way a woman looks at the thing she's been expecting and already has a plan for.

"You can't stop it," she said. "The signal is running. The minds are taken. You broadcast hope, some of them wake, then they sleep again. The device is still here. The countdown is running. In four hours, the signal becomes permanent. And the city is mine."

"I'm not here to stop the signal," Kara said.

"Then what're you here for?" Indigo said.

"I'm here for you," Kara said.

She moved.

The battle was fast and brutal. It was everything three weeks had been building toward — the work, the watch, the paper, the pattern, the names, the leak, the device, the city, the hope, the fear, the choice. And the person on the other side was a woman who'd been working from the inside and had no problem killing anyone who stood in her way.

J'onn went for Indigo. He'd been stabbed by her. He had the wound. He had three hundred years. He went at her with Martian speed and Martian anger, and she was fast, she was smooth, she was the thing that had been working from the inside.

But J'onn was a Martian. And a Martian who's been fighting for three hundred years doesn't stop.

He caught her. Lifted her. Ripped her apart.

Indigo was gone.

Kara went for Non.

He was standing by the device. He'd designed it. He'd run it. He'd lost his wife and carried her dream and decided to destroy a city to finish what she'd started. He was standing there with the thing he believed, and he was going to run the countdown, and he was going to make the signal permanent, and he was going to do it because he thought it'd the only way.

"You had a choice," Kara said. "You had the device. You had the power. And you chose to use it on a city of people who didn't ask for it. And you called that finishing what Astra started. And Astra wouldn't have wanted this. She wouldn't have wanted the dead. She would have wanted the living to be free."

"She would have wanted the world saved," Non said.

"She would have wanted the people free," Kara said.

And then she moved.

The Kryptonite blade — the one Indigo had used, the one that had been in the fortress — was on the table. Kara grabbed it. She ran at Non. He met her. They moved through the device room, the signal humming through the walls, the countdown running, the city behind them waking or sleeping or some of each, and Kara didn't stop.

She drove the blade into Non.

He stopped. He looked at her. The thing behind his eyes shifted. The calm broke. And what came out wasn't anger. It was what he'd been carrying for a long time — the grief, the loss, the thing that had been driving everything, the wife he'd lost, the dream he'd been carrying, the mission he'd been completing.

And then he was gone.

Kara stood in the device room. The device was still running. The signal was still going out. The countdown was still running.

And Indigo had been right.

"The signal can't be stopped," the device said. The machine. The thing built to run. "Once it's running, it can't be stopped. The countdown is running. In four hours, the signal becomes permanent."

Kara looked at the machine. Looked at the device. Looked at the Kryptonian structure around her — the fortress, the prison, the base, the thing built to hold and keep and run.

And then she had the idea.

Not the signal. Not the device. The place.

Fort Rozz was a Kryptonian fortress. Built to hold things. Built to keep things out. And it'd running the signal. And the signal couldn't be stopped.

But the signal could be moved.

Kara picked up the device. Lifted it. Held it to her chest — the Kryptonian metal, the machine, the thing running the city — and walked out of the room.

Out of the fortress. To the edge of the cliff. She looked up.

The sky was clear. The stars were there. The vacuum of space was above her.

She flew.

Up, higher and higher, the device in her arms, the signal running through it, the city below her waking and sleeping and some of each. She flew until the city was a map and the ocean was a sheet and the world was a ball hanging in the black.

And then she let go.

The device. The signal. The fortress. She released the whole thing. The machine. The Kryptonian metal. The signal running through it. The thing that couldn't be stopped.

She watched it drift away into the dark. Away from the city. Away from the people. Away from every mind that had been taken.

The signal faded. The city stopped. The rhythm was gone. The people on the street looked around and didn't know what had happened. But they were awake. All of them. Every one.

And Kara was alone.

She'd let go. She'd released the thing that couldn't be stopped. She'd moved it into the vacuum where it could run and run and run and touch no one. And now she was alone, floating in the black, the stars around her, the Earth below her, not holding anything.

She floated.

Arms at her sides. Eyes open. Stars there. And she wasn't moving.

The signal was gone. The device was gone. The fortress was gone. She was alone in the vacuum, not holding on to anything, and she didn't know if she could get back.

***

Alex had been watching the screen.

The DEO tech room. The console. The signal tracker. The thing Max had been tracking since the moment it went out. She'd watched the signal move — from the city to the fortress to the sky. She'd watched it go up. She'd watched it go into the black.

And she'd watched Kara go after it.

"She let go," Alex said. "She released it and she let go, and she's floating, and she's not moving, and she's not coming back."

Eliza was beside her, hand on Alex's shoulder. She'd been the one who'd found the frequency. The one who'd spoken into the signal. And now she was looking at the screen, at the thing Kara had done.

"She's not coming back," Alex said.

Adrian was at the console. The paper open. The names, the times, the pattern. The parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code. The work. He'd been holding it for three weeks.

And Kara was floating in the black.

The device was gone. Indigo was gone. Non was gone. The signal was in the vacuum, running and running and touching no one. The city was awake. The people were back. And Kara was floating, not moving, not holding on to anything.

Adrian looked at her on the screen. Looked at the paper on the console. The names. The pattern. The work.

The work had been about the device. About the leak. About the pattern the device couldn't change. He'd held it because the device could take a mind but couldn't take paper. He'd held it because the ordinary had to exist somewhere, and the paper was the one place it still did.

But the device was gone. The signal was in the black. The city was awake. And Kara was floating.

And Adrian thought: if the device had worked — if it had taken every mind, if it had made the city a river with one current — then what would the ordinary be?

What would the band on his wrist be?

What would the paper be?

He'd chosen ordinary.

He'd sealed himself off from what he'd been born as.

He'd built a life around a free mind in a free city.

And the device was the thing that would have made that choice a joke.

A life without a world to live it in.

An ordinary without anyone to be ordinary for.

It hadn't worked. The city was awake. Kara was here, floating in the black, and Alex was standing next to him saying she wasn't coming back.

And Adrian realized the ordinary wasn't something he had.

It was something he did.

The choice to keep holding when the reason for holding was gone.

The thing he was doing right now — reading the tracker, reading the paper, looking for the thing that would bring her back.

Not because the device was here.

But because the device could come back.

Because the pattern was still here.

Because the ordinary meant nothing if it couldn't survive the thing that threatened it.

He looked at the screen. Looked at the signal tracker. Looked at the thing Max had built, the thing that could track a Kryptonian pod.

"There," he said.

"What?" Alex said.

"The pod," Adrian said. "Kara's pod. The one she used to get to the fortress. It's on the tracker. It's near her. She has a pod nearby. She can use it to get back."

"She's not moving," Alex said.

"She's not moving because she let go," Adrian said. "And she's floating. And the pod is near her. And she can use it. But she's not moving. She's not reaching for it."

Alex looked at the screen. Looked at Kara, floating in the black, the stars, the Earth below.

And Alex ran.

Out of the tech room. Down the corridor. Out of the DEO. To the pod bay, the thing Kara had used to get to the fortress, the thing that was near her sister in the black. She got in.

She launched.

Up through the atmosphere, the pod cutting through the air, the black above her, the stars, the thing she'd watched on the screen. She flew until she saw Kara.

Kara was floating. Eyes open. Arms at her sides. Not moving. Not holding on to anything.

Alex reached out. Grabbed Kara's arm. Pulled her toward the pod. Held her — the sister holding the sister, the one who'd been taken and come back holding the one who'd let go and hadn't come back.

"You're not alone," Alex said. "You're not alone. You're not alone."

Kara's eyes shifted. She looked at Alex. Looked at the pod. Looked at the stars.

And she held on.

Alex flew them back. Down through the black, through the atmosphere, through the air. Landed at the DEO.

She carried Kara out of the pod. Laid her on the bed in the medical room.

Kara was breathing. She was cold. She was the sister who had let go and hadn't come back, and she was breathing.

Eliza was there. She put a hand on Kara's shoulder. Looked at Alex, and the look was what a mother gives a daughter who's gone into the black and brought back the thing the mother couldn't reach.

"You brought her back," Eliza said.

"I brought her back," Alex said.

***

The pardon came three days later.

General Lane had been the one to sign it. He'd stood at the barricades and opened them. He'd watched the city wake. Watched the signal go into the black. Watched Kara float in the vacuum and Alex fly into the black and bring her back. And he'd signed the thing that had been hovering for months — the DEO's secrecy, the legal problem for J'onn.

J'onn was reinstated. Back at his desk. The Martian Manhunter. The man who'd been fighting for three hundred years and hadn't stopped. The Kryptonite was out of his system. The wound was healing.

He stood in the DEO in front of the whole team. Winn. James. The receptionist. Eliza. Alex. Kara on the couch, blankets around her, the cold still in her bones. Adrian at the console, the paper still open, the pattern still on the screen.

J'onn looked at them. At Kara. At Alex. At Eliza. At the people who'd been holding the city while he was hurt, the people who'd been holding the pattern, the people who'd been waking the city, the people who'd gone into the black.

"I've been fighting for three hundred years," he said. "And I've been fighting for this city. And I've been fighting for the people in this room. And I'm not done."

He looked at Kara.

"You asked me once what I was," he said. "I told you I was a Martian. And I'm. But I'm also the thing you gave me. The thing you showed me. The choice. The hope. The thing a control device can't replicate. And I'm not done fighting. Because you're not done. And the city isn't done. And there's always the next thing."

Kara looked at him. She'd let go. She'd released the device. She'd floated in the black. And now she was on the couch, blankets around her, the cold still in her bones, and the thing J'onn was saying was the thing she'd known since the day she arrived on Earth — the choice.

She nodded.

***

Cat called her into the office the next morning.

Kara walked in. Cat was at her desk, looking at the city through the window. She didn't look up when Kara came in.

"Sit," she said.

Kara sat.

"I've been watching you for a year," Cat said. "I've watched you save the city. Save the plane. Save the building. Save the people. I've watched you hold the pattern on paper and watch the parking lot at two in the morning and do the work no one saw. I've watched you walk into a fortress and release a device that couldn't be stopped and float in the black and not come back. And I've been watching you for a year, and I haven't said the thing I'm about to say."

She looked at Kara.

"You're not a sidekick," Cat said. "You're not an assistant. You're not a person who watches and waits and does the work no one sees. You're Supergirl. You're the hero. You're the thing this city has been waiting for. And I'm not going to watch you do the work no one sees anymore. I'm promoting you. To senior correspondent. To the thing you've been for a year and haven't been named. And I'm naming it. Because you're Kara. And you're Supergirl. And the city knows it now."

Kara looked at her. Didn't say anything. Didn't cry. She sat there and felt the thing that had been the reason she'd become Supergirl — the work, the pattern, the paper, the names, the leak, the watch, the thing no one saw — and she felt it named.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me," Cat said. "Come to work. You have a city to cover."

***

James met her in the bullpen.

He had the camera. The lens. He'd been the photographer for a year, the man taking pictures and holding the thing he'd been trying to say. And now he was standing in front of her with the camera, and the thing in his hands was the thing he'd been holding for a year.

"I've a picture for you," he said. He held up the camera. The screen showed the photo he'd taken — Kara on the roof, the city behind her, the cape behind her, the thing she was, the thing she'd always been.

"I took it the day you flew into the storm," he said. "The day you saved the plane. I took it and I didn't show it to you. Because I didn't know how to say what it meant. And now I know."

He looked at her. Looked at the photo. Looked at the city through the window.

"I know what you're," he said. "And I know what you're not. And I don't care. I care about you. And I've been caring about you for a year, and I'm not going to stop. And I'm not asking you for anything. I'm showing you the photo. And I'm telling you what it means to me."

Kara looked at the photo.

Looked at James.

She looked at the bullpen.

Winn at his desk with the paper and the pattern. The receptionist at the front desk. The chipped mug on her own desk. The theater mask magnet from Adrian's desk — the one Evelyn had left behind.

The small thing that had been sitting there since the day Evelyn left it.

The cartoon bandage in her pocket.

The ordinary things that had been the texture of her life for a year.

She looked at the man in front of her who'd been the thing she hadn't been able to have and hadn't been able to name.

She didn't say anything. She reached out. Took his hand.

And then she closed the distance. Stepped into him, and the bullpen and the work and the pattern on the screen fell away, and there was only the woman and the man and the year of things unsaid. She kissed him — not a thank you, not a promise. The kiss of a person who'd stopped waiting for the right moment and decided the moment was now.

He held her. The camera was in his other hand, the photo forgotten. The thing between them was the thing it had always been — not the secret, not the hero. The woman and the man. The choice.

They stood there, in the bullpen, in the middle of the work, the pattern still on the screen, the city awake behind the window.

***

That night, they had dinner at the DEO.

Winn had brought the pizza. James had brought the drinks. Alex had brought the salad. Eliza had brought the thing she'd been making for weeks. Adrian had brought the paper — the pattern, the names, the times, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code — and he put it on the table beside the pizza.

"This is the work," he said. "This is the pattern. This is what we've been holding. And I'm not stopping."

"You can stop for one night," Kara said.

"I'm not stopping," Adrian said. He looked at the paper. Looked at the people around the table. "I'm not stopping because when the device was running, and the city was gone, and every mind was taken — the ordinary was gone. The life I chose was gone. Not the band on my wrist. Not the desk. The thing the band and the desk existed for. The free mind in a free city. That was gone. And the only thing left was this paper. The only thing left was the pattern the device couldn't reach. I held it because it'd the only thing still alive."

He paused.

"And I'm not stopping, because the device isn't coming back today. But it came back once. And it will come back again. And the next time, I want the paper to be here. I want the pattern to be here. I want the names to be here. Because the leak can't change paper. And the ordinary — the thing I chose — survives only if someone holds it when the device is trying to take it away."

Kara looked at the paper. Looked at the people around the table. Looked at the city through the window — the lights, the people, the thing that had been taken and had come back, the work, the pattern, the hope, the choice.

She raised her glass.

"To the city," she said.

"To the city," they said.

"To the people," she said.

"To the people," they said.

"To the work," she said.

"To the work," they said.

She drank. Looked at the paper. Looked at the people. Looked at the city. And she felt the thing she'd felt since the day she arrived on Earth — the hope, the choice, the thing a control device couldn't replicate.

And she held it.

***

The pod was on the roof of the DEO the next morning.

Kara's pod. The one that had brought her to Earth. The one that had taken her to the fortress. The one that had been near her when she'd floated in the black. The one Alex had used to fly into the vacuum and bring her back.

It was sitting on the roof, the Kryptonian metal, the thing she'd found the day she arrived, the thing she hadn't known what it'd.

Kara stood on the roof and looked at it. The pod was open. The seat was there. The controls were there. The thing that had brought her to Earth was there, open, waiting.

She walked to it. Put her hand on the edge. Looked inside. Looked at the seat. Looked at the controls.

She didn't get in.

She stepped back. Looked at the city. The people were walking — not in the rhythm, in their own steps. The rhythm was gone. The signal was gone. The device was in the black, running and running and touching no one. And the city was hers.

She looked at the pod. Looked at the city. Looked at the sky.

And the pod was there, open, waiting.

She didn't know what it meant. She didn't know if it'd the thing that had brought her to Earth or the thing that was going to take her away. She didn't know if the next thing was the city or the black or the thing she hadn't known.

She turned.

Walked off the roof. Went back to the DEO. Went back to the work. The paper, the pattern, the names, the times, the parking lot, the back gate, the ghost code, the leak. The thing she'd been holding for three weeks. The thing she'd been holding since the day she arrived on Earth. The hope. The choice. The thing a control device couldn't replicate.

She held it.

And the pod was there, on the roof, open, waiting.
