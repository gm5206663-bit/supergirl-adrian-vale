# Season 1, Episode 3 — Fight or Flight

## Chapter 6 — Under the Headline

Cat set her phone on the roof of the car, checked that it was recording and looked up at Supergirl.

“Where are you from?”

“Krypton.”

The answer changed Cat's expression less than Kara had expected. She asked about the journey, the powers, the years between arriving and stepping into public view. Kara gave her enough to explain why helping mattered, but not a street, a family name or the names of the people who had taken her in.

“And during those years?” Cat asked. “You watched disasters on television?”

Kara glanced toward the city. “Sometimes.”

“Knowing you could do something?”

“Not knowing whether I could do enough. That's different.”

Cat made a small note. Kara wished she could see it.

They talked about the harbor. Kara named the crewman who had directed the tanker and explained why the tug had mattered. Cat listened without interrupting, then asked whether the masked man had taught her to fly.

“No.”

“Superman, then?”

“I've had help. That doesn't make everything I do someone else's decision.”

Cat considered her for a moment. “And what do you want when you aren't pulling people out of burning buildings? A home? Children? Someone waiting for you?”

The question caught Kara in a place the others had not. Hours earlier, she had stood outside a room where her mother's sister was learning what kryptonite did. For years she had wanted family back. Now some of it was here, and she had no answer simple enough for a magazine.

“Do you ask my cousin whether he plans to settle down?”

Cat's pen stopped.

Kara heard what she had said a fraction too late.

“Your cousin.”

She could not take the word out of the recording. She could decide how much more of her life followed it.

“Yes. But I'm not giving you his private life, or mine.”

“I'm asking about yours.”

“You can ask about what I do here.” Kara met her eyes. “I came because I wanted people to hear from me. Not because everyone I love agreed to become part of the interview.”

For the first time, Cat set the pen down.

“Then tell me what you want them to understand.”

Kara looked beyond her at the lights along the waterfront.

“That I want to stay. Even when I get something wrong.”

***

By breakfast, the word *cousin* was on every screen in the café.

Alex watched the clip without touching her coffee. Kara had already told her how it happened, which made sitting through the edited version slightly less terrible than being caught by it.

“There's a difference between keeping your address private and giving everyone a map of who can hurt you,” Alex said.

“I know.”

“You know now.”

Kara looked down at the headline. There was no mention of the crewman, or the tug, or the answer about staying. Those parts would come in the full article, Cat's announcement promised. The family connection had gone out first.

James stopped beside their table on his way to work. He asked whether she was all right, and Kara managed to answer while becoming inconveniently aware that he had remembered how she took her coffee. Alex waited until he left before raising an eyebrow.

“Don't,” Kara said.

“I haven't said anything.”

“You're thinking loudly.”

Alex smiled, but it faded when the clip began again. “We'll talk about the interview properly at the facility. Henshaw wants to review what went out.”

“Is Astra—”

“Still there. Still asking to speak to you.”

At CatCo, Adrian placed one of two small bakery bags on the edge of Kara's desk while she was sorting the messages that had arrived overnight.

“Glazed,” he said. “The other one is mine.”

She looked into the bag and found a pastry tucked beside the donut. For a moment, it was possible to remember a morning when that would have been the most interesting thing waiting for her.

“Thank you.”

He glanced at the television above the neighboring desks. “Does Alex know?”

“I called her before Cat's announcement.”

“Good.”

He did not tell her that he would never have made the mistake. She was grateful enough for that to tear off a piece of the pastry before Cat called her name.

The article would lead the next magazine issue. There would be a launch reception, photographs and guests who would need places to stand while telling one another they had understood Supergirl's importance before anyone else did.

Kara received the guest list. Adrian received the room and production requirements.

“The interview is finished,” Cat told them. “That is not an invitation for the rest of you to begin improvising.”

***

At the DEO, the holding corridor had acquired a second checkpoint.

Kara passed it with Alex and stopped outside Astra's room. Her aunt was seated at the table, upright despite the suppression field. On the adjacent monitor, one of her guards was eating under observation. Neither had been moved into a cell with the other prisoners.

Astra looked toward the glass before the speaker came on.

“I wondered whether you would keep your promise.”

“I said I would listen.”

“Then come in.”

Alex touched the control panel. “You can speak here.”

Astra's gaze shifted to her, then returned to Kara. “Do you accept every condition they set?”

“No. I don't refuse them just because they're human, either.”

For a moment Kara saw the woman she remembered: the impatient tilt of the head, the certainty that a sufficiently clear explanation would make everyone else understand. Then Astra looked toward the emitters and the memory became harder to hold.

“I asked the program from my pod about you,” Kara said.

“And did it sound like your mother?”

“Yes.”

“That isn't the same as hearing everything she knew.”

Kara had spent too much of the night wondering about that already. “Then tell me something I can check.”

Astra leaned forward. “Keeping me here doesn't mean everyone outside will stop moving.”

“Who is giving orders?”

“Non won't wait forever for word from me.”

Kara's hand tightened against the ledge. “He's on Earth?”

“Yes.”

“Then give us a way to speak to him before someone else gets hurt.”

“First take this weapon out of my room.” Astra glanced toward the emitters.

Kara felt Alex become still beside her. “I'm not turning it off as the price of a conversation. You sent someone to take an agent. We're trying to prevent another attack.”

Her aunt studied her. “Would you have asked me these questions before he brought you to the warehouse?”

“Kael didn't choose my questions,” Kara said. “I went there because your man told us where you were. I would have asked who you were sending after people whoever found you first.”

Alex remained beside the console, where Kara could see her without looking away from her aunt.

“I wanted to speak to the girl who used to ask me why the stars had names,” Astra said.

“She's here.” Kara put her hand against the ledge beneath the glass. “She's also asking you about the plane.”

Astra leaned back.

The silence lasted until Kara understood that it was the answer she was going to receive today.

Henshaw met them beyond the checkpoint. He had a report open on his tablet and two agents waiting for instructions. The new prisoners had not made the facility safer simply by standing on the other side of a door.

“We've reviewed your interview,” he told Kara. “We can't retrieve a public relationship once it's published. From now on, we plan for someone choosing targets around it.”

“I didn't give her anyone's address.”

“You gave her a reason for someone to start looking.”

An alert sounded at the operations desk. Several vehicles had collided on the freeway, with a bus on its side across two lanes. Kara was moving before the dispatcher finished reading the location.

Alex followed her to the door. “Keep the channel open.”

“I will.”

***

The bus driver was trying to push a steering column away with both hands when Kara reached the broken windscreen.

“Stop for a second,” she said. “Let me take the weight.”

She braced the damaged frame, bent the obstruction clear and helped the driver through the opening. Beyond the bus, people were climbing out of cars while a police officer directed them away from leaking fuel. Kara carried the driver to the waiting paramedics and turned back for the passengers still inside.

The first blast struck the road between her and the bus.

Concrete burst upward. She shielded her face, heard someone scream and saw an armored figure descending through the dust. Light burned at the center of his chest and along the machinery fitted to his arms.

“Superman's cousin,” he said.

Kara put herself between him and the people at the ambulance.

“You need to move away from them.”

“I need him to know what it feels like.”

A second blast hit her before she could get another word out. It drove her into the side of an abandoned car. Metal folded around her shoulder; she caught the door as it came loose and used it to turn the next discharge away from the ambulance.

“Alex. Armored attacker. He's talking about my cousin.”

“I heard. Backup is being called. Get the civilians clear.”

Kara threw the ruined door into the weapon on his right arm. Its outer casing cracked. He staggered sideways, then pointed the other gauntlet toward the bus.

She reached the vehicle first. Bracing against its frame, she turned the broken windows away from him while the last passengers crawled toward the far exit. Heat struck the road behind her. She could hear the man shouting over it, demanding that she turn around and face him.

There were people beneath her hands. She did not let go.

***

Alex's call reached Adrian while he was checking the reception layout.

He asked for the location and the direction in which the attacker was firing. Then he took the case from his work bag and used the private changing room he knew was empty. The seal released beneath his fingers. The fitted black suit, gloves, boots and mask followed; the coat settled around him as he checked that nothing of his ordinary clothes remained visible.

He left by the sheltered service route, climbed clear and accelerated toward the freeway.

The scene resolved into moving parts before he landed: a burning patch of road, people crouched behind vehicles, Kara holding the bus steady, a man rotating his damaged weapon toward an ambulance.

Adrian entered from the man's uninjured side.

The gauntlet turned. Adrian slipped inside its line of fire, caught the forearm and carried the motion upward. The discharge passed into empty sky. He pivoted behind the armor before the attacker could bring the damaged arm across, folded both weapon-bearing arms away from the crowd and lowered him to one knee.

A short burst from the flight apparatus lifted dust beneath them. Adrian changed his stance with it rather than wrenching the metal backward. The man's feet returned to the road. His arms did not move.

“Stop,” Adrian said.

The attacker tried again. Nothing useful happened.

Across the lanes, Kara helped the final passenger clear the bus and set the vehicle down. When she looked up, the fight had become something else: a furious man held still by someone who had not needed to hit him.

Alex's voice came through her earpiece. “Winn passed a clear image to James. He recognizes the armor. He says the name is Reactron.”

“Human?” Kara asked.

“According to the earlier reports. Don't break open the chest unit. We're checking what powers it.”

Kara crossed the road. Beneath the cracked plate on the gauntlet, light pulsed along an exposed connection. Adrian was watching it too.

“His right arm was damaged before I arrived,” he said, keeping his voice low. “The chest housing is intact.”

“Get off me,” the man spat. “This isn't about you.”

“Then stop aiming at the people who aren't part of it,” Kara said.

He looked at her, his face twisted by an anger that did not belong to this road or this morning.

“Ask your cousin about my wife.”

***

Henshaw did not send the guards from Astra's corridor to the freeway.

He sent the previous incident reports to Alex and put her in contact with the city's hazardous-materials commander. The attacker was human; responsibility for his arrest would go through the human authorities. An unknown energy system capable of injuring a Kryptonian was still something the DEO could help them understand.

“You have the technical desk,” he told Alex. “I need the containment teams here.”

She nodded and returned to the feed.

On the road, Adrian had moved Reactron behind the empty bus, away from the gathering cameras, without uncovering his face. The damaged armor was still energized. He kept its weapons directed clear while Kara worked with the police to widen the perimeter.

There was no need to pull the chest apart simply because they could.

A technician talked them through the existing images of the suit. Adrian asked for a closer view of the undamaged side, then compared it with the mechanism in front of him. The design was unfamiliar; the distinction between a weapon feed and the housing that kept its power source contained was not.

“Keep that section assembled,” he told Kara when she came back. “We want the power unit out without turning the man wearing it into the opening in its casing.”

Alex confirmed the danger from the old reports. Lead shielding was part of the answer. The city had equipment coming, and Lord Technologies had offered a suitable shielded transfer container from one of its laboratories.

The offer arrived with a request to know who would acknowledge the company's contribution.

“Tell them I will,” Kara said. “When the container is here.”

She went to meet the vehicle rather than wait for it to work through the stopped traffic. Adrian remained with the prisoner. The ambulance crews reported that the last passengers were clear. The containment team could now work without anyone trapped beside the damaged vehicles.

Reactron stopped struggling long enough to look at the mask.

“What do you get out of this?”

Adrian watched the damaged connection instead of answering immediately. It was holding at the same level. There was no reason to disturb it.

“Right now? Those people get to go home.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

“No.”

Kara returned with the container and the team's instructions. The hazardous-materials officers stayed at the edge of the cleared area with their instruments. Alex kept the technical channel open, passing on what had been verified rather than making Kara guess at an unseen component.

Adrian released one arm only after it had been made safe and secured. He used that hand to support the damaged outer frame while Kara positioned the shielding. His movements were small, exact, without the careless pressure that could have buckled the housing.

Kara waited for the technician's confirmation before separating the power unit. For a moment, its weight settled into her hands. She kept it inside the shielding, lowered it into the container and held it steady until the closure was secured.

The light in the armor went out.

Without it, the man on the road looked abruptly smaller. The gloves and plates remained, but they no longer translated every movement into a threat to the people around him.

The radiation team's readings fell. Only after the officers cleared the approach did the arrest team come forward.

Kara stayed beside the ambulance while they examined the prisoner. Adrian stepped away from the officers' cameras and left masked, taking a route that did not point anyone toward his place of work.

A reporter called after Kara as she prepared to go.

“Did Superman help you stop him?”

She looked back at the road: the empty bus, the medics, the people waiting to learn whether they could retrieve their cars.

“No,” she said. “A lot of people here did.”

***

Winn had put a kettle in the old office.

Kara noticed it before the monitors. It sat on a cabinet beneath a stack of paper cups, beside a chair he had apparently rescued from somewhere else in the building. James was looking over the equipment when she came in, and Alex stood at the desk with a folder open in front of her.

“This belonged to Ed Flaherty,” Winn said. “The room, not the kettle. Nobody's using it.”

“Does Cat know you're using it?” Kara asked.

“She knows I'm sorting the spare equipment. Which I am.”

Kara looked at the screens. One showed public traffic reports; another held a map of the freeway. “Keep anything private off the shared systems.”

“Already doing that.” Winn indicated the closed laptop beside him. “And I lock up.”

Alex turned the folder around.

“Ben Krull. He was at a reactor incident Superman stopped. His wife didn't survive. Krull did, and he decided that made your cousin responsible.”

Kara studied the photograph. Without the armor, he looked like someone she could have passed in a grocery store.

“Was my cousin there when she died?”

“The reports say he stopped a much larger disaster,” Alex said. “They don't say he could save every person inside it.”

That distinction would not have brought a woman back to her husband. It also had not put any of the people on the freeway in his debt.

James touched the watch on his wrist.

“He gave me a way to reach him,” he said. “If things go wrong.”

Kara looked up. “Were you going to use it?”

“I was watching the footage and waiting for someone to tell me you were alive. Of course I thought about it.”

“I'm alive.”

“I know that now.” His hand left the watch. “I didn't know what help you already had.”

Kara remembered Alex telling her that keeping someone uninformed did not keep them safe. “Then we should get better at telling each other. If someone is in danger, call for help. I just don't want to find out afterward that everyone made a plan around me except me.”

James nodded.

Winn looked between them. “I can put a contact list on the private machine. Numbers people have agreed we can share. No civilian names on the field channel.”

“Ask first,” Kara said.

“I heard the agreed part as I was saying it.”

She smiled and took the cup he offered. The room was not impressive. Its chairs did not match, and one monitor needed a folded piece of cardboard under its stand. It was still a place where she could sit down without pretending none of this belonged to her life.

***

Cat's draft arrived with six instructions about punctuation and one about finding a better photograph.

Kara read it twice.

The harbor error was there. So was the freeway rescue, including the crew and the technicians she had asked the reporter to acknowledge. But the headline made Superman the first person in the story, and much of the article treated Kara's life as an answer to the question of what his family would do next.

She took the pages into Cat's office.

“This makes it sound as if she's inherited a business.”

“She wears the family emblem, shares the family powers and has confirmed the family connection. It would be negligent not to ask what she intends to do with it.”

“But she told you.”

Cat looked up.

“In the interview,” Kara added. “You quoted it here. She wants to help.”

“Everybody wants something flattering in print.”

“She also admitted what she got wrong.”

“Yes.” Cat took the pages and found the paragraph Kara had marked. “Which is why that answer is in the piece.”

Kara had come prepared to argue against someone who refused to see any good in Supergirl. It was harder to argue with someone who had included the good and still chosen an angle she disliked.

“Could the headline at least begin with her?”

For a moment Cat did not answer. Her eyes moved from the marked paragraph to Kara's face, staying there long enough to make Kara aware of the glasses against her nose.

“You're very invested in this.”

“You asked all of us to be.”

Cat's mouth almost moved into a smile. “That was a better answer.”

She crossed out two words in the headline, not the whole thing.

“Now go make sure the reception has chairs. I would hate to lose a major advertising account because we were too busy having convictions to provide furniture.”

Outside, Adrian was sorting the final deliveries. He had changed privately and re-engaged the seal before returning to his desk; the closed case was back in his work bag. Kara handed him the revised page and let herself breathe.

“Successful?” he asked.

“Two words.”

He checked the change. “That's two more than you had when you went in.”

She leaned against the edge of her desk. “Astra says Non is here. Alex is putting together what we can actually check.”

Adrian's attention sharpened. “Did she give you a way to reach him?”

“No. Or a place. Just that he won't wait forever.”

“I'll keep the contact line available.”

Kara nodded, then looked down at the article again. “Krull asked me to ask my cousin about his wife.”

Adrian stopped arranging the delivery slips.

“Alex told me what happened,” Kara continued. “What the reports say, anyway.”

“Did he ask about the people on the road?”

“No.”

For a few seconds Adrian looked at the papers without reading them.

“I remember being more interested in who had failed me than in who was standing in front of me,” he said quietly.

Kara did not pretend she had forgotten what he meant.

“Does it help to know that now?”

“It changes what I do next.”

He picked up the delivery slips again. The answer did not make the earlier part of his life less terrible. It did make sense of the care with which he had held the damaged armor while people worked around him.

Kara looked down at Cat's amended headline, then folded the page so the ink would not smudge.

***

By the following evening, the reception was ready to begin.

For the first hour, the worst emergency was a projector that refused to recognize the presentation laptop. Adrian replaced the connection while Kara redirected a photographer who had mistaken a sponsor's name for an invitation to interview everyone in the room about their personal lives.

Winn found the missing cartons of magazines behind a stack of equipment cases. By the time Cat turned to ask where they were, he and Kara had put them on the display tables.

“You saved me,” Kara whispered.

“I'm putting that on my next performance review.”

He asked her to dance once the room settled. She accepted. For one song, he stopped checking his phone and she stopped watching the doors.

Across the room, Maxwell Lord congratulated Cat on the issue and made certain she knew whose laboratory had provided the containment equipment at the freeway.

“I read the report,” Cat said. “So did everyone you sent it to.”

“Good equipment deserves good publicity.”

“Then you'll be delighted that I named the technicians too.”

Lord smiled as if she had done him a favor. He asked what she knew about the man in black.

“Less than I intend to.”

“Two powerful newcomers, an agency nobody wants to explain, and a man wearing a reactor on a public road. You don't find the pattern interesting?”

“I find your interest interesting.”

Nearby, Adrian set a replacement microphone on the presentation desk and continued past them. Lord's gaze followed him only long enough to see that he was staff. The black case was not in the room, and nothing on Adrian's wrist had slipped beyond his cuff.

James joined Kara after the dance and handed her a glass of water.

“I talked to your cousin,” he said quietly. “After we knew it was over.”

“About the freeway?”

“About you being all right. The rest is yours to tell him.”

She nodded. It was a small distinction, but she could feel the effort he had made to leave it in place.

He looked toward Lord, who was still speaking to Cat. “He's going to want to know how the suit worked.”

“So will the people who have to keep it from hurting anyone else.”

“Those might not always be the same questions.”

Kara watched Lord smile for a photograph. She was beginning to understand that a problem could stop firing at people and still have quite a lot of life left in it.

The last guests left after ten, leaving empty glasses and tired staff. Adrian helped collect the last equipment cases while Kara put aside the copies Cat wanted delivered in the morning.

For once, they left work carrying only the things they had intended to bring home.

***

Lucy Lane was in James's office the next morning.

Kara stopped in the doorway with a copy of the magazine still in her hand. James stood up and introduced them. Lucy's smile was warm and direct; she knew where to put her bag and how close to stand beside his desk without asking.

“Lois's sister?” Kara asked.

“Among other things.”

Kara felt herself flush. “Sorry. That came out—”

“I'm used to it.” Lucy glanced at James. “I wanted to catch him before work swallowed the whole day.”

“Right. Of course.” Kara placed the magazine on the desk and left them to it.

In the corridor, she heard Lucy say she did not like how they had left things. James's answer was quieter. It was still easy to hear when she did not turn her attention elsewhere.

Kara made herself keep walking.

Whatever had happened between them had begun before he came to National City. A few good conversations and a borrowed hope did not tell her what it was, or give her a claim on how it ended.

Behind the closed office door, James asked Lucy whether she was staying in National City.

“For now,” she said. “Would you have called if I hadn't come?”

He looked down at the magazine Kara had left. “I kept deciding it wasn't the right time.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“No,” he admitted. “It isn't.”

They agreed to dinner that evening. Neither called it a fresh start. There was too much of the old one still waiting to be said.

***

At her desk, a message waited from Kara's cousin.

*James says you're all right. I'd rather hear it from you.*

She sat down and typed back that she was. Then she told him what Krull had said, and asked about his wife.

The answer took longer than the first message.

He remembered her, he told Kara. He had tried to reach everyone. Knowing that did not make the people he could not reach stop being people he remembered.

Kara rested her hands above the keyboard.

*I didn't stop him alone,* she wrote.

*I know. You stayed to get the people out. I'm glad you had help.*

She read that twice before answering.

***

That evening, Alex brought food to Kara's apartment and found the magazine open on the table.

“She moved your answer up,” Alex said, pointing to the paragraph about staying.

“I had to argue.”

“I'm shocked.”

Kara handed her a carton and sat beside her. They ate for a while without discussing any of the people behind the doors at the DEO. Then Kara told her about Lucy.

Alex listened without making a joke at James's expense or promising that his past would turn out to be unimportant.

“Did you have a good time with him?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Then that part was real. It doesn't have to tell you everything yet.”

Kara looked toward the magazine. The public had acquired a cousin for her. She had acquired an aunt in a holding room, a friend whose past she was still learning how to face, and a clearer idea of how little she knew about the people she liked.

Outside, a siren began to rise.

She listened long enough to hear the direction, then picked up her phone and checked the alert before standing.

“I'll call it in,” she said.

Alex moved the food out of reach of the cape. “I'll keep this warm.”

Kara left the magazine where it was. There would be another headline tomorrow. Somewhere below, someone needed help before it could be written.
