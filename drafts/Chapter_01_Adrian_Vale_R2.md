# Season 1, Episode 1 — Pilot

## Chapter 1 — Adrian Vale

Kara had been given someone to look after.

With the ground shaking beneath her parents’ feet, that was the part she could understand. Her little cousin would need her. Earth would be strange, and Kal-El would be too young to remember the home they were leaving. She could tell him about it. She could make sure he wasn’t alone.

She climbed into her pod carrying that small, necessary certainty.

Through the closing canopy, she watched her mother’s face. Then the launch pressed her into the seat, and Krypton fell away beneath her.

She never caught up with Kal-El’s ship.

The destruction of their world threw her off course and into the Phantom Zone. Outside her pod, there was no reassuring approach of another morning. Twenty-four years passed elsewhere while she remained the child who had promised to protect a baby.

When she finally reached Earth, that baby was Superman.

He found her a home with the Danvers family, scientists who had helped him understand his own abilities. There was a bedroom, a place at the table, and a girl named Alex who had not expected to become an older sister.

Kara learned English. She learned which sounds she was supposed to hear and which objects she was supposed to find heavy. She learned to catch herself before doing something that would leave everyone staring.

The Danvers family became her family. That part was real, however much she missed the people she had lost.

But the task she had crossed space to perform was gone.

Years later, in National City, she could still remember exactly how it had felt to be needed.

***

Cat Grant called while Adrian was standing in line at the bakery.

“Tell me you’re already in the building.”

“I’m already in the building.”

A pause. Behind the counter, the woman reaching for his coffee glanced up.

“Was that the truth?” Cat asked.

“You asked me to tell you.”

“Vale.”

“Two blocks away. You moved the meeting to nine yesterday.”

“And now it’s earlier. Bring the Tribune figures.”

“I have them.”

“Then bring yourself.”

The call ended. Adrian slipped his phone into his coat pocket and accepted the coffee.

“Trouble?” the woman asked.

“Employment.”

She smiled and passed him a paper bag. Her eyes lingered on his face before she turned to the next customer.

National City was already warm beneath its yellow sun. A delivery truck blocked half the street, its driver unloading boxes with patient disregard for the horn behind him. At the crossing, Adrian waited beside a man arguing into a headset.

A narrow band rested beneath his left cuff. Dark metal, an unobtrusive face, a clasp fitted to his wrist. Most people took it for an expensive watch.

He had built everything beneath that assumption.

The voice beside him, the truck engine and the pedestrian signal were distinct. Beyond them, the city became ordinary noise. No conversations through walls. No distant machinery resolving into individual moving parts. His own footsteps, when he crossed, kept pace with everyone else’s.

He could remove that restriction.

This morning, he finished his coffee on the walk to work.

***

Kara was trying to end a telephone call without dropping a stack of folders when he reached the executive floor.

“The revised version,” she said into her headset. “Yes, I know you sent one. There was another revision after that.”

A blue folder slid free. Adrian caught it against the edge of her desk before it reached the floor.

Kara mouthed a thank-you, listened for another few seconds, and closed her eyes.

“Please send both. I’ll sort them out.”

She ended the call.

“Conference room?” Adrian asked, lifting the folder.

“That one. The others are for Cat.”

“Do you want a hand?”

“I want an email that means what it says.” She separated the stack and gave him two more folders. “But I’ll settle.”

He put his bakery bag on the desk while he adjusted his grip. Kara looked at it.

“Almond?”

“You can have it.”

“Did you already eat?”

“I bought two.”

She accepted the bag. “Then thank you.”

As he carried the files away, Winn rolled his chair back from his workstation.

“Still up for a movie tonight?” he asked her.

Kara shook her head. “I told you. I have a date.”

“I was allowing for a change of plans.”

“No change.”

“Okay.” He tried to make the word sound more casual than it came out. “Have fun.”

“I’m going to try.”

She took a bite of the pastry just as the private elevator opened.

Cat emerged, passed an opinion on the smell inside it, and crossed the floor without slowing. Kara set the bag down and followed her with the folders. Adrian collected the Tribune report from his desk and joined them.

Cat’s office overlooked a city whose newspapers were selling fewer copies of it every year.

She put her handbag down. “How bad?”

Adrian opened the report at the marked page. “Circulation is down. The latest advertising forecast doesn’t cover the present staffing costs.”

“I asked how bad, not how delicately you could describe it.”

He turned the report so she could read the figures.

Cat scanned them. Then she slid a list toward Kara.

“Prepare the termination letters.”

Kara looked down at the names. “For the Tribune?”

“Unless we’ve acquired another newspaper since I left the elevator.”

“All of these people?”

“That is generally what a list means.”

Kara held the page without moving. “The Daily Planet isn’t doing this.”

“The Daily Planet has Superman.” Cat tapped the report. “People buy a newspaper when they believe something extraordinary might be inside it. Metropolis has someone obligingly providing extraordinary every week. We have a declining subscription base.”

“Some of these people have worked there for years.”

“I can read their employment records, Kara.”

Adrian waited until Cat looked back at him.

“I’ll check the vacancies elsewhere in the group before HR sends anything.”

“Check,” Cat said. “Don’t promise.”

“I won’t.”

“And don’t manufacture a position because you dislike delivering bad news.” She turned to Kara. “The new art director has the layouts I asked for. Go and find out why I’m still waiting.”

Outside the office, Kara stopped beside Adrian’s desk.

“How many vacancies are there?”

“Not enough.”

Her hope faltered, though she nodded. “Tell me if you need the personnel files.”

“I will.”

He sat down with the list. She went to collect the layouts.

***

The photograph caught Kara’s attention before the man did.

Superman hung above the world, his cape lifted behind him. She had seen the image reproduced so often that finding a print within reach felt oddly personal.

“That one keeps following me,” a voice said.

She turned. The man beside the desk was tall, broad-shouldered and smiling at her with an ease that made her briefly forget why she had come.

“You took it?”

“James Olsen.”

“Oh.” She looked from him to the photograph. “Of course. I should have—Kara. Danvers. I’m here for the layouts.”

He handed her a folder without making her feel foolish about the introduction.

“You used to be at the Daily Planet.”

“I did.”

“With Superman.”

“He wasn’t usually at a desk.”

She laughed, and some of her embarrassment eased.

“What made you move?”

“A chance to do something different.” He glanced at the photograph. “He thought coming here might be good for me.”

It was a peculiar experience, hearing a stranger refer so casually to her cousin. To most people, Superman belonged to the sky, or the news, or an argument about what the world should expect from someone who could do the impossible. James spoke of him as a friend who offered advice.

Kara looked back at the photograph. “Was he posing?”

“Not for that one.”

“It doesn’t look like he knows you’re there.”

“Sometimes that’s when you get the best picture.”

James studied her for a moment, then lifted the framed print and offered it to her.

She blinked. “I can’t take that.”

“I have other prints.”

“Still.”

“You like it.”

That was difficult to dispute. She accepted it carefully, the layouts tucked beneath her other arm.

“There’s something familiar about your eyes,” James said.

Kara’s fingers tightened on the frame.

“Superman,” he added. “A little.”

“Oh. Well.” She pushed her glasses up. “I think his are probably better photographed.”

His smile gave her no clear indication of what he meant. Before she could decide whether to ask, her phone vibrated with a message from Cat.

“The layouts,” she said. “I really should—”

“Go. I’m still new enough to want her to like me.”

Kara left with the folder and the photograph, smiling despite herself.

***

By the afternoon, Adrian had found two openings worth referring to HR and rejected a third that would have required a copy editor to become a qualified accountant by Friday.

He sent the useful information without pretending it solved the Tribune’s problem.

Cat kept him through a call about costs. When it ended, she corrected one line in his notes and handed them back.

His cuff shifted as he reached for the paper.

“New?” she asked.

He followed her glance to the band. “No.”

“An expensive-looking watch with nothing useful on the face. How reassuringly conventional of you.”

He pulled his sleeve down. “I try to fit in.”

“Try to get that to legal before they leave.”

In the corridor, a junior employee turned too sharply with an armful of framed prints. The top frame struck a door handle and fell.

Glass cracked against the floor.

Adrian stopped.

For a moment, he remembered the windows of his parents’ home under Krypton’s red sun. His mother disliked the light falling across the table at that hour. His father kept promising to have the shading adjusted, then forgot until she complained again.

Daren Varr could oversee difficult work and command a room full of people who wanted something from his house. A small domestic repair had defeated him for weeks.

Ilyra had laughed about it.

Adrian could remember that laugh without effort. He did not need the sound of breaking glass to bring it back.

What the sound brought was everything afterward.

His father had belonged to one of Krypton’s influential houses. His mother had been born into a branch of Daxam’s royal family. Neither standing had protected them from Daxamites who considered their marriage an offense that had to be punished.

They had been murdered for choosing each other.

He had killed people who had nothing to do with their murders.

He remembered streets on Krypton, not Daxam. Buildings opening under the force he brought against them. Faces turning toward him. The destruction had gone far beyond the people responsible for his parents’ deaths, until a city had become something survivors would spend their lives describing.

His power had already been extraordinary under the red sun. Its development had also damaged his ability to come back from anger. He knew how quickly that narrowing could happen: a thought, a certainty, then action before anything else seemed to matter.

Knowing it now did not change what he had done then.

Kryptonian technology had finally restrained him. Fort Rozz had followed.

“Mr. Vale?”

He was standing in a corridor at CatCo.

The employee crouched beside the broken frame. Blood marked the side of one finger.

Adrian knelt, careful of the fragments.

“Leave the glass.”

“I’m sorry. This was supposed to go in Ms. Grant’s—”

“We can replace the frame.” He took a clean handkerchief from his pocket. “Hold this against the cut.”

She accepted it. He moved the unbroken prints away from the debris and called maintenance.

The band remained beneath his cuff. He had built it to restrict what his body could do, not what he could remember.

When the employee went to wash her hand, he picked up his folder and took it to legal.

There was still work to finish.

***

At home, Kara put the photograph where she could see it, then spent several minutes trying not to look at it while she chose something to wear.

Alex found her holding two outfits and looking dissatisfied with both.

“The blue,” Alex said.

“You haven’t even seen the other one properly.”

“I’ve seen you decide you hate it twice.”

Kara lowered the other outfit. “Fair.”

Alex sat on the bed while Kara changed. She listened to the account of Cat’s morning, the Tribune list and the arrival of James Olsen.

“You sound happier about the last part,” she observed.

“He was nice.”

“And?”

“And very good-looking. I’m allowed to notice.”

Alex smiled. “You’re also supposed to be going out with someone else tonight.”

“One date. Which might be good.” Kara checked her reflection, then turned away from it. “I want something to be good.”

“Your life isn’t bad, Kara.”

“I know. That’s not what I mean.”

She sat beside her sister. “Today I carried a list of people back to my desk and tried to work out how you tell someone they aren’t needed anymore. Then I collected layouts. Then I made calls. And tomorrow I’ll do more of it.”

“Those things matter to somebody.”

“I know they do.” Kara looked at her hands. “But I keep thinking there’s something else I’m supposed to do.”

Alex’s expression changed, just enough for Kara to recognize it.

“We’ve worked hard for you to have this life.”

“I’m not saying I don’t want it.”

“Then don’t talk as if it’s nothing.”

Kara looked up. “That isn’t what I said.”

For a few seconds, neither spoke. Alex reached across the small space between them and straightened a fold at Kara’s shoulder.

“You look good,” she said.

Kara let the argument rest. “What time is your flight?”

“Soon enough that I should leave.”

“Geneva. Conference. Very glamorous.”

“Mostly uncomfortable chairs.”

Alex picked up her bag. At the door, she turned back.

“Have a good evening.”

Kara promised to try.

***

Adrian bought tomatoes on the way home.

The grocer insisted the smaller ones were better. Adrian disagreed, tasted the slice he was offered, and bought the smaller ones.

At his apartment building, a neighbor was struggling to lift a wheeled shopping bag over the threshold. He held the door and raised the front of the bag for her.

“Late again,” she said.

“Am I?”

“I notice these things.” She inspected his groceries. “At least you’re cooking. Too handsome to live on sandwiches.”

He laughed and carried the bag as far as the elevator before she insisted on taking it back.

Upstairs, he changed out of his work clothes and washed the tomatoes. His apartment was comfortable without being impressive. Books occupied more space than ornaments. A half-repaired lamp stood at one end of the dining table, beside a box of tools he had not yet put away.

He opened the kitchen window while the water heated. Traffic drifted up from the street, mixed with someone’s music.

His phone lay on the counter within reach.

There were evenings when he still expected an interruption from a life that had no use for this room. Tonight, he cut vegetables, adjusted the heat beneath the pan and discovered that he had bought too much bread.

He wrapped half for the following day.

***

Kara’s date had spent more time looking around the bar than looking at her.

She tried another question. He gave her an answer that did not invite a second one, then excused himself.

A little later, above the ordinary noise of the room, she heard him asking a waitress for her number.

Kara looked down at her drink.

At least she could stop wondering whether she was being unfair.

She was considering whether to finish it when a news bulletin appeared on the television. An aircraft climbed through the evening sky with fire streaming from an engine.

Flight two hundred and thirty-seven.

Geneva.

She stood so abruptly that the chair caught against her leg. On the screen, the aircraft was losing height.

Alex was on that flight.

Kara left without waiting for her date to return. Outside, she hurried past people who had not yet looked up and turned into an alley.

She could hear the aircraft now, though hearing it did not make the distance any smaller.

For years, concealment had been a succession of small choices. Walk instead of run. Reach instead of leap. Pretend not to hear.

She bent her knees and pushed upward.

Her first attempt went wrong. Brick scraped beneath her hands, and she dropped back toward the pavement, catching herself before she fell.

Above the buildings, the engine noise changed.

Kara looked up again.

This time she rose past the rooftops.

***

The cabin shook around Alex.

People were crying out. A woman across the aisle gripped the seat in front of her while the man beside her tried to fasten a belt his shaking hands would not let him manage.

Alex leaned across and helped him. Then the aircraft lurched, driving her back into her own seat.

She could identify the danger. That did not give her any way to stop it.

Outside, the city lights were getting closer.

Below the aircraft, Kara reached for the fuselage. The first contact jarred through her shoulders. She had known it would be heavy; she had not understood how difficult it would be to move something so large without turning it the wrong way.

She pressed upward. The descent slowed, but not enough.

Wind flattened her clothes against her. Ahead, a bridge crossed the water, its structure filling more of her view with every second.

Kara shifted her hands and changed her angle. The plane resisted the correction. For a frightening moment she thought she had made it worse.

Then the nose began to come around.

She held the pressure, watching the space they had to pass through. Steel swept close above the wings. Beyond the bridge lay open water.

She brought the aircraft toward it as carefully as she could.

The landing sent spray high around the fuselage and drove a cry out of her. She stayed with it through the violent drag of the water until the motion diminished and the plane settled.

It remained afloat.

In the cabin, Alex heard the sounds around her change. People were still frightened, but now they were asking questions, reaching for one another, realizing that they had stopped falling.

She looked through the window.

Kara was on the wing.

For a moment, Alex could do nothing but stare at her sister in the gathering darkness. Then she saw a passenger raising a phone.

Other people were looking too.

Kara glanced toward the shore, where help was approaching. A moment later, she lifted away from the aircraft and disappeared into the night.

***

Adrian was washing a saucepan when his phone rang.

He dried one hand and answered.

“Turn on a television,” Cat said.

“What happened?”

“A woman just prevented an aircraft from becoming tomorrow’s casualty figures. I’d like us to cover the story while it’s still tonight.”

He went into the living room and switched on the screen.

An aircraft rested on the water. Emergency boats surrounded it, their lights moving across the wet fuselage. The report cut to distant footage of a figure beneath the plane.

Adrian watched without speaking.

The picture was poor. He could distinguish a person bracing against the aircraft and struggling to correct its descent, but not a face.

“Adrian?”

“I’m here.”

“Transport and overnight staffing. Find out what footage is available, and let legal handle the rights. Nobody promises money without approval.”

“Do we have confirmation everyone got out?”

“They’re reporting survivors. Keep checking.”

He found a pen and wrote down the assignments.

After Cat hung up, he called the transport coordinator, then the night editor. The editor was trying to assemble a reliable passenger account from several contradictory reports. Adrian arranged a car for a reporter rather than make the man interrupt the call to do it himself.

On the television, passengers were being brought ashore. One stood wrapped in a blanket, both hands covering his face.

The rescue footage played again.

By the time Adrian had learned there was an aircraft in trouble, it was already on the water. The woman in the blurred recording had reached it in time.

He watched her hold on through the last correction.

Then he returned to the calls. The night desk needed verified names, a driver and someone who would answer a phone. Those were things he could provide now.

The saucepan stayed in the sink.

***

Kara reached her apartment wet, breathless and smiling so hard her face hurt.

She shut the door and stood with her back against it until a laugh escaped her. Then another. She had been frightened from the moment she recognized the flight number until the aircraft stopped moving, but beneath that fear had been something she had missed for years.

She had known what to do.

Not every movement. Not how to carry a plane. But why she was there, and why she had to try.

She turned on the news and found the aircraft on the screen. The footage was already repeating. A reporter was talking about a woman who could fly.

When the knock came, Kara hurried to the door.

Alex stood outside.

Kara pulled her into a hug before either of them spoke. For a few seconds, Alex held her just as tightly.

“You’re okay,” Kara said against her shoulder.

“I’m okay.”

Kara stepped back, still holding her arms. “I saw the report, and I knew it was your flight, and I didn’t even think about—did you see? I actually did it.”

“I saw.”

Alex’s tone made her stop.

Her sister looked past her at the television. A small, indistinct figure rose away from the aircraft.

“Everyone saw.”

“There were people on that plane.”

“I was one of them, Kara.”

“Then why do you sound angry?”

“Because you don’t know what happens now.” Alex shut the door behind her. “That picture is going everywhere. People are going to ask who you are. They aren’t all going to be grateful.”

Kara let her hands fall.

“I wasn’t thinking about a picture.”

“I know. That’s what scares me.”

The excitement that had filled the apartment a moment earlier had nowhere to go. Kara folded her arms, suddenly conscious of her damp sleeves.

“What was I supposed to do?”

Alex looked at her.

“Tell me,” Kara said. “I knew you were up there. I could help.”

“I’m glad you did.” Alex’s voice softened. “But that doesn’t mean it’s safe to keep doing it.”

“So this is the only time?”

“We need to be careful.”

“We’ve been careful for years.”

“And you’ve had a life.”

Kara glanced at the screen. Passengers were stepping into waiting boats. A child clung to someone’s neck.

“I want this to be part of my life too.”

Alex shook her head. “You don’t understand what you could be putting at risk.”

“Then explain it to me.”

For a moment, Kara thought she would. Alex’s mouth opened, but the explanation did not come.

Kara felt the absence more sharply than another warning.

She looked away. “I’m tired.”

“Kara—”

“Please. I’m glad you’re safe. I just don’t want to argue anymore tonight.”

Alex waited, then nodded. At the door she turned back, searching Kara’s face for something neither of them seemed able to say.

After she left, Kara stood in the quiet apartment.

The news kept playing. The woman in the recording was difficult to see, but Kara remembered the weight against her hands, the bridge coming toward her and the moment the aircraft had finally stopped.

She sat down, pulled a blanket over her wet clothes and watched the passengers come ashore.
