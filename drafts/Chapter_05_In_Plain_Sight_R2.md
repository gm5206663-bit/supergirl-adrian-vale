# Season 1, Episode 2 — Stronger Together

## Chapter 5 — In Plain Sight

*Earlier that week*

James brought the cape to the roof in a plain box.

Kara recognized the weave before he explained where it had come from. She ran her fingers across fabric made on a world that no longer existed, and for a moment the city below them seemed impossibly far away.

“My cousin kept this?”

“He kept the blanket he arrived in. This was made from it.” James watched her lift it carefully from the box. “He encouraged me to come here. But leaving Metropolis was my decision. I wanted a life that wasn't just standing next to his.”

“And now you're standing next to mine.”

“If you'll let me.”

She looked up at him, then down at the red cloth spread between her hands. It was a gift, not an instruction. She could accept it without deciding that being saved had made her own efforts unnecessary.

That afternoon, Alex called with a direct number for DEO operations. Henshaw had agreed to let Kara work with them. She would share her movements; they would share information that could keep her alive. Training would begin when Alex cleared her arm.

Adrian left the facility that evening after another interview. Henshaw required further appointments and contact details, but did not attempt to turn voluntary cooperation into an arrest whose consequences he could not control. The conviction remained under investigation. There was no pardon, and Adrian did not ask for one.

At home, he closed the seal himself. Distant traffic faded into the ordinary sounds of his apartment. Then he set an alarm for work.

For several days, the arrangement held.

***

The missile found Kara just as she decided she had lost it.

Its reflected flare appeared in the curve of the canyon wall. She rolled, felt the heat pass her shoulder, and climbed hard enough that the ground became a map beneath her. A second missile came up from below. This time she waited, let it commit to its turn, then dropped between two ridges and heard it detonate against empty stone.

Alex's voice sounded in her ear. “Better.”

“That sounded almost like a compliment.”

“It was. Don't make me regret it.”

Kara grinned and banked toward the observation station. Her arm no longer hurt; the torn costume had been repaired, and the new cape followed her turns without coming apart. For the first time since the power plant, flying felt like something she could enjoy rather than something she had to survive.

Then Winn called about the harbor.

She passed the location to Alex before changing course. By the time she reached the waterfront, black smoke had swallowed the roofs of two loading sheds. Flames ran along a fuel-slicked stretch of pier toward an oil tanker. Men were still coming down its gangway.

Kara landed beside a stack of pallets, drew breath and blew toward the fire.

The result was immediate and wrong. Flame flattened, then spread beneath a parked trailer. A firefighter shouted a warning as burning debris skittered across the concrete.

She stopped. “Winn, that made it worse.”

“I heard. I'm calling Adrian. Stay on.”

The tanker had to move. Kara flew to its side, planted her hands beneath the curve of the hull and began to lift. Steel groaned above her fingers. She stopped at the sound, but the plate had already started to dimple.

“Not there.”

Adrian was beside her, one hand against the hull, not lifting it. He had arrived without a coat, his office shirt bright against the smoke. His sleeve had ridden up above the disengaged band.

“The ship can float,” he said. “Let it.”

On deck, a crewman was pointing toward the bow. Kara followed his gesture and saw the lines holding the vessel against the quay. Beyond the smoke, a tug was trying to reach the far side.

She flew up to him. “What do you need?”

This time she waited for the answer.

The crewman shouted instructions into a radio, then pointed her toward a heavy mooring line. Kara carried it clear while Adrian went to the bow. With the remaining berth lines released and the tug taking the stern, they began drawing the tanker away from the burning pier. Kara could feel the difference through the rope: steady resistance instead of metal yielding beneath her palms.

A section of gangway collapsed behind them.

Adrian was gone from the bow before the people on it reached the water. He caught one man under each arm, crossed the gap and set them beyond the fire crews. Then he returned for a third who had fallen against the rail.

Kara kept the line taut. The tug's horn sounded; the crewman gave her a broad sweep of his arm. She changed direction with him, and the tanker turned slowly into open water.

By the time the last line came free, foam was covering the burning slick along the pier. The ship had a damaged plate and frightened crew, but its cargo remained inside it.

Kara landed beside Adrian while an ambulance attendant examined the men he had carried out. She started to thank him, then noticed where he was looking.

Across the cordon, a television cameraman had turned his lens toward them.

There was nowhere to step that would undo the last few minutes.

Adrian looked at the camera, then at the rescued men. He stayed until the attendant finished asking his questions.

***

Cat had the harbor footage paused when Adrian returned.

His face filled half the screen. Smoke curled behind him; one sleeve was blackened nearly to the elbow. The image was much clearer than anything recovered from the aircraft.

“Close the door,” she said.

He did. Through the glass, he could see Kara stop beside her desk, then deliberately turn toward Winn instead of pretending not to watch.

Cat pressed play. On-screen, Adrian crossed a gap above the water carrying two men. She let the clip run to its end before looking back at him.

“Were you planning to mention this at your annual review?”

“No.”

“Clearly.” She folded her hands. “Are there more of you on my payroll?”

“I can speak for myself.”

“Then do that. Is the name on your employment documents the name you use?”

“Here, yes.”

Cat's expression sharpened at the qualification. “We'll return to that. Did you bring this emergency to my company?”

“No. Winn called because people needed help.”

“And you can fly.”

“Yes.”

For the first time, she glanced at his wrist. The movement was small, but he saw it. Observation was not understanding; it was still more than she had possessed that morning.

“I'm not going to make you perform in front of a board meeting,” she said. “But I am going to report something half the waterfront watched happen. You may decline to discuss where you come from. You may not give my newsroom a false explanation and expect me to print it.”

“I won't.”

“Good. The employment questions will take longer. For now, change your shirt. You're shedding someone else's warehouse on my carpet.”

She stopped him at the door.

“And Adrian? Saving those men was worth doing.”

He turned back.

“That doesn't answer my other questions.”

“I know.”

Outside, his telephone was ringing. So were two neighboring telephones. A producer who had known him for months stood up as he passed, uncertain whether to congratulate him or ask him to lift something.

Adrian went to his desk. The guest schedule was still open on his screen, every appointment exactly where he had left it. Nothing else about the room felt the same.

At the afternoon editorial meeting, Maxwell Lord appeared on the wall monitor condemning untrained intervention around hazardous cargo. The tanker had not spilled, but the first burst of fire had made an excellent loop for television.

Cat watched it once. “Now that we've all seen how easily a rescue becomes a headline about liability, perhaps we can ask Supergirl what she's actually trying to do.”

James shifted in his chair. Cat noticed.

“An interview, James. With her. Not a statement from her cousin, not commentary from the gentleman who apparently has been moving my office furniture by hand as an act of personal restraint.”

Adrian kept his eyes on the agenda.

Kara did not quite manage the same thing.

***

The stinger lay in a clear evidence tray beside a dead guard's identification card.

Kara had been called to Plastino Chemicals the next morning. Alex led her past the broken security door and showed her the puncture in the guard's uniform before uncovering the recovered projectile. Its color pulled a memory out of Kara with startling force: her mother arriving home late, putting down her case, answering a child's question about the prisoner she had sentenced that day.

“A Hellgrammite,” Kara said.

Henshaw looked up. “You're certain?”

“I remember my mother describing one. The stingers. The way his mouth changed.” She studied the torn cabinet behind Alex. “Did he take chemicals?”

“Several containers. We're comparing this with other thefts.”

On the drive back, Alex let her examine the photographs instead of telling her to leave the investigation to professionals. Kara found she could be grateful for that and still hate the sight of the guard's card. He had been smiling when someone took the picture.

At the DEO, Alex took her to the training room.

“There are low-level kryptonite emitters in here,” she said before opening the door. “They're off. If you agree, we'll use them to put us close enough physically that I can teach you without your accidentally throwing me through the wall.”

Kara looked through the glass. “You weren't going to tell me that last week, were you?”

Alex did not try to smile it away. “I'm telling you now.”

Kara considered the room, then nodded.

Knowing the weakness was coming did not make it pleasant. Her knees felt less certain beneath her. When Alex told her to attack, she lunged too directly and found herself on the mat with her sister holding her wrist.

“Again,” Alex said, helping her up.

The second time, Kara tried harder. That only gave Alex more momentum to use. The third time she stopped watching her sister's hands and noticed the shift of weight that came before the throw. She still went down, but she understood part of what had happened.

Sweating and furious, she sat on the edge of the mat.

“Someone stronger can still walk through all of this.”

“Yes,” Alex said. “But that isn't a reason to give the person in front of you every opening you can.”

Kara looked at the faint mark where the axe had cut her. It was nearly gone.

“Show me the turn again.”

***

Astra watched the harbor footage in silence.

The man beside her had brought it as useful information about the human authorities. He stopped explaining when she leaned closer to the screen.

She knew that face.

For an instant she remembered the prison corridor, the officers taking care not to stand too close to a sealed door, and a man who had listened to her account of everything Krypton refused to hear. He had argued with her. He had also refused her, with none of the hesitation she was accustomed to overcoming.

“Leave that,” she said when the soldier reached for the controls.

On-screen, Kael-Varr stood beside her niece in the clothes of an ordinary human. The camera caught him turning toward a question. There was no mistaking him.

Astra's fingers tightened on the table. Vartox's silence acquired a new explanation, though not yet a proven one.

“Empty this location,” she said. “People first. Equipment we can carry. No attacks on the girl.”

The soldier stared at her. “The scavenger—”

“Bring him here before we leave.”

The Hellgrammite arrived hungry and resentful, flanked by two soldiers. Astra had intended to use him to take one of the humans investigating the escaped prisoners. Now she put a small telephone on the table instead.

“You will watch the people searching for you. Tell me where they take their prisoners. You do not bring anyone back here.”

“I need food.”

“Then find it without leading them to us.”

He looked toward the departing soldiers, the half-empty room. “What's changed?”

Astra turned the screen so he could see it.

His expression emptied. He took one step backward and struck the soldier behind him.

“Not him.”

“Then remember what I said.”

She watched him take the telephone with a hand that had begun to shake. There would be no useful prisoner if fear drove him into a blind attack. Nor would there be an army worth commanding if she offered its people to Kael one at a time.

Before she left, Astra watched the last seconds of the recording again. Her niece stood near him without fear.

She wondered how much Kara knew.

***

“Is Supergirl supposed to do nothing until she can do everything perfectly?” Kara asked.

Cat looked up from the proposed front page. “Is that what I said?”

“No, but—”

“Then don't improve my argument into something easier to object to.” She tapped the harbor photograph. “She saw a fire and tried to put it out. Admirable. She made it worse. Less admirable. Then she listened to someone who knew how to move a ship, and things improved. There's a lesson in there that doesn't require her to retire.”

Kara glanced at the second photograph, the one of Adrian.

“What if people decide they don't need her?”

“People needed three different emergency services, a tugboat and two flying people yesterday. I doubt we're in danger of having too much useful help.” Cat handed back the page. “Tell James I still expect an answer.”

Kara found him near the elevators. A few minutes later, Winn joined them in the alley behind the building and stopped when he saw who was already there.

“You know?” he asked James.

James looked from him to Kara. “You know?”

“Yes,” Kara said. “And now we can stop having every conversation twice.”

Winn glanced upward, checking the windows. “That would save a lot of time.”

She wanted their help finding things she could do well, and learning the things she could not yet do well. Not a campaign that turned every rescue into a test of whether the city liked her. James knew people who worked on the ground. Winn could find calls that needed a faster response than the city could provide.

That evening, James directed her to a stranded ambulance before its patient ran out of time. Winn found a frightened child whose pet was stuck in a tree. He neglected to establish that Fluffy was a snake until Kara already had it draped over both arms.

By the time she returned it, she was laughing.

Later, carrying groceries up six flights for an elderly woman whose elevator had failed, Kara realized she had not thought about the harbor for nearly an hour. The woman offered her an orange. Kara accepted it and ate it on the roof while James listened to her account of the snake.

When the laughter faded, he told her about Cat's latest demand.

“I came here because I wanted to do work people could call mine,” he said. “Apparently I brought my most marketable friendship with me.”

“You don't want to ask him.”

“I don't want you to think you owe me an interview because someone can make my job unpleasant.”

Kara turned the orange peel between her fingers. “Cat already has that passenger recording. And now she knows another person in her office can fly.”

James looked toward the lit windows of CatCo. “Then I can't promise she won't see you.”

It was not comforting, but she trusted it more than reassurance he could not support.

“I still want people to hear from me,” she said. “Let me work out how.”

***

Alex found the link in the laboratory results: chlorine-based biology, with the stolen pesticides serving as food rather than the components of a bomb.

By the following afternoon, a map of the thefts covered the briefing-room screen. The DEO could move a shipment through a route the Hellgrammite had already used, but Henshaw no longer treated the expected arrival of one escaped prisoner as proof there would be only one attacker.

“Vartox had orders,” he said. “We still haven't identified who gave them. Assume someone may be watching this.”

Kara stood beside Alex instead of outside a closed door. Adrian was across the table, attending one of the appointments he had agreed to keep. He had returned to the seal after the harbor, but Henshaw had asked whether he would assist if they drew the prisoner out.

Adrian studied the proposed route.

“That crosses two open slip roads.”

“We can close them,” Alex said.

“And the truck?” Kara asked. “If he attacks it, someone should be there who can keep the chemicals off the road.”

Henshaw adjusted the assignments. The truck would cross an isolated service stretch under surveillance, with the approaches closed to traffic. Alex would travel in the escort vehicle; Kara would cover the cargo and crews. Adrian would stop the attacker before he could reach the crews or escape. He would release his seal before deployment, not wait for someone to be hurt.

It was less tidy than Henshaw's first diagram. It also gave them an answer to more than one thing going wrong.

As the others collected equipment, Kara stayed beside the map.

“Are you going to keep helping?” she asked Adrian.

“When I agree to something, I'll do it.”

“That isn't quite what I asked.”

“No.” He looked through the glass at the agents checking their gear. “I'm not looking for a new uniform. I still wanted the job I went to yesterday morning.”

“I know.”

“Cat has scheduled a meeting about whether I can keep it.”

Kara winced. “She's been asking James for an interview with me.”

“She asked me too. I told her it was your answer to give.”

Outside, Alex raised a hand: time to go.

Kara moved toward the door, then looked back. “Thank you for that.”

Adrian nodded and drew back his cuff.

***

The first stinger struck the escort's windscreen rather than the cargo truck.

Alex ducked behind the reinforced dashboard as a second projectile punched through the side window. It grazed her upper arm. She ordered the driver to brake and dropped out behind the door, keeping the vehicle between herself and the direction of the shots.

The Hellgrammite landed on the roof. His hand reached toward the shattered passenger window.

It never reached the metal.

Adrian caught his wrist, lifted him clear of the vehicle and set him on the road with his arm held behind him. The entire movement was over before Alex could bring her weapon around the door. She kept it lowered when she saw who had him.

Beyond them, the cargo trailer swerved. Another stinger from the opening attack had punctured a tire. Kara reached the truck before its rear wheels crossed the edge of the road, bracing against the chassis where the load was carried rather than pushing on the thin side of the tank. The driver kept the brakes engaged while she brought it to a stop.

The prisoner strained once against Adrian's grip. Nothing moved except his own shoulder. He twisted his head to see who had caught him.

All the movement went out of him.

“Kael.”

Alex approached with restraints, blood darkening the tear in her sleeve. Adrian glanced at the wound, then back at his prisoner.

“Stay still.”

“I didn't come for you.” The words stumbled over one another. “She told me not to come near you.”

“Who?” Alex asked.

His eyes did not leave Adrian. “Astra.”

Kara heard the name from beside the truck. She stayed where she was until the driver confirmed he had the vehicle secure. Only then did she cross the road.

The prisoner saw her emblem and began speaking faster.

“I was supposed to watch them. The humans. I needed the chemicals. I didn't know you'd both be here.”

“Your hunger doesn't explain the dead guard,” Alex said.

He looked away from her. “They won't let me eat in your prison.”

Henshaw arrived while the agents secured him. He listened to the request before answering.

“We'll establish what you need to stay alive. That isn't a bargain for testimony.”

Adrian handed the prisoner over when the restraints and transport team were ready. He did not follow him into the enclosure.

On the road beside Alex lay a telephone that had fallen from the prisoner's coat. Its screen lit up before the evidence officer could bag it.

***

They answered on the second call, after moving into the mobile command post and setting the device where their own camera could record it.

Henshaw stood beside the technician. Alex had wrapped her arm; Kara waited close enough to see the screen. Adrian remained several steps behind her, away from the prisoner's line of sight.

The call offered video.

Henshaw looked at Kara. “You don't have to speak.”

“If it's her, I want to.”

The technician accepted.

At first there was only a dark background and the edge of a shoulder. Then a woman looked into the camera, and Kara forgot the questions she had been arranging in her head.

Knowing her aunt might be alive had not prepared her for the movement of her face.

“Astra.”

The woman went still. Whatever she had expected to see, it had not been this.

“Kara?”

Kara stepped closer. There were differences from her mother's face that she had remembered imperfectly: the bearing, the expression around the mouth, the way Astra studied a person as if preparing to dispute their answer.

“You survived,” Kara said.

“So did you.” For a moment Astra's voice lost its command. “I saw the aircraft. I hoped—”

“Was that your order?”

The question stopped her.

Behind Kara, no one moved. The technician was sending the connection details for tracing; Alex watched the screen rather than her console.

Astra's expression closed. “There is more happening here than you understand.”

“Then explain it. There were people on that plane. My—people I love could have died.”

“I did not call to speak before human interrogators.”

“Your man attacked them. Before that he killed someone for getting in his way.”

“He was not sent to kill a guard.”

“But you sent him.”

Astra looked past Kara as Adrian moved close enough to enter the camera's view.

There it was again: the stillness Kara had seen in Vartox and on the road. It was less naked in her aunt, contained inside years of discipline, but it changed her face.

“Kael-Varr.”

“Astra.”

“So this is where you went.”

“I took a job.”

“You could have found us.”

“I wasn't looking.”

The hurt in Astra's expression was quickly overtaken by anger. “And now you stand with Alura's daughter and the people building another prison.”

“I'm standing beside someone who asked you a question.”

“I wanted to speak to my niece.”

“Then speak to her.” Adrian stepped out of the camera's view. He remained beside the table, close enough that Astra had seen him take only a step.

Kara held the edge of the table. “What do you want here?”

“To prevent another world from destroying itself while its leaders debate whether anything is wrong.” Astra leaned closer. “Your mother refused to listen. I won't watch it happen again.”

Kara remembered the guard's identification card. She remembered men on a burning gangway, and a tanker that had needed someone to listen before trying harder.

“If saving people means you don't have to listen to them, how do they tell you to stop?”

“You sound like her.”

The resemblance hurt more than an insult would have.

“I miss her,” Kara said. “I missed you too. That doesn't make this all right.”

For a moment, she thought Astra might answer without reaching for the argument again. Instead, her aunt looked toward something beyond the camera.

“We cannot speak freely like this.”

“Then stop sending people to hurt us.”

The screen went black.

The connection had not lasted long enough for the requested carrier information to come back. When it did arrive, it identified only a broad search area. Henshaw sent a team to start checking it.

Kara stared at her own reflection in the dark screen until Alex touched her uninjured shoulder.

This time, when Kara turned, her sister was right beside her.

***

The Hellgrammite gave them a warehouse address.

Adrian reached the address ahead of the first team and met them at the loading doors. The building was empty. Dust marked places where heavy equipment had recently stood, and packing material lay across the floor, but no one answered when the agents called out.

They found no prisoners. No hidden people called for help. Adrian checked the spaces the team could not safely reach while the agents photographed the abandoned equipment.

Astra had begun leaving before the trap was set.

By the time he returned to collect his things, Alex had cleaned and dressed the cut on her arm. It was shallow. Kara was waiting outside the medical room, looking at the emblem on her own chest.

“She wanted you on her side,” she said.

“She often did.”

“She was afraid of you. She still asked.”

“Yes.”

Kara looked up. “My mother must have believed putting people away was the right thing. Astra believes whatever she's doing is right. And you're the one who told me most of the people you killed hadn't done anything to you.”

Adrian sat on the bench opposite her. He did not offer her a way to make the comparison comfortable.

“I can't decide what your aunt will choose,” he said. “Neither can the person she was when you were a child.”

“Do you think she'll stop?”

“Not because I frighten her. Today she changed how she approached us. That's different.”

Kara nodded slowly. Fear had prevented a fight; it had not made her aunt agree with anything she had said.

Across the corridor, Alex came out with her sleeve rolled above the dressing.

“I have something to show you,” she told Kara.

Adrian rose. “I'll see you tomorrow.”

“At work?” Kara asked.

“If Cat hasn't reassigned my desk to someone who stays on the ground.”

She smiled, briefly but without forcing it. He left her with Alex and closed the seal before passing the outer checkpoint. By the time the outside door shut behind him, the conversations inside were no longer within his hearing.

Alex took Kara down a quieter corridor.

The room at its end contained a projector and space enough to stand before it. When the light rose, Kara nearly said the same thing she had said in her apartment.

Then the image of Alura turned toward her.

“This one can answer,” Alex said softly. “It's an interactive program from the technology in your pod. Not the recording I brought you.”

Kara stepped forward. The image waited.

There were a hundred questions she wanted to ask, and one request she knew the light could not grant. She held her hands together until the urge to reach for her mother passed.

“Tell me about Astra,” she said. “Not only why you sentenced her. I want to understand what happened before that.”

Outside, Alex found Henshaw watching through the observation window.

“Thank you,” she said.

He nodded without turning it into an explanation. After Alex went back to her sister, he remained alone in the corridor for a moment. In the dark glass, his reflected eyes shone red.

Then he turned away.

***

Cat was in her car when James called.

“If you are about to tell me that Supergirl values her privacy,” she said, “I would like you to consider how little that sentence improves my evening.”

“She agreed to meet you.”

“Where?”

The car rose smoothly from the ground.

Cat went silent. Outside her window, the city dropped away; beneath the chassis, a red cape streamed in the air. When the car settled again, it was in a quiet place beyond the crowded streets, with enough light for Cat to see the woman who stepped around to her door.

Kara waited while Cat got out. She could have sent another message through James. She could have let Adrian become the subject everyone discussed until the questions about her grew less urgent. Neither choice would have made the things she wanted to say less necessary.

Cat looked her over with an attention she never wasted on an unimportant story.

For an instant, Kara wondered whether the first word out of her mouth would be her name.

Instead, Cat held up her phone.

“May I record this?”

“Yes.”

“And are you speaking for your friend as well?”

“No,” Kara said. “He can answer for himself.”

Cat's thumb hovered over the screen. “Then let's hear from you.”

Kara stood a little straighter in the cape her cousin had sent her. She knew the others were out there: her sister, James, Winn, Adrian. Knowing she could call them did not make this their decision.

It made it easier to make her own.
