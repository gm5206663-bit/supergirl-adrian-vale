# Season 1, Episode 1 — Pilot

## Chapter 1 — Adrian Vale

Cat Grant called while Adrian was standing in line at the bakery.

“Tell me you’re already in the building.”

“I’m already in the building.”

A pause.

“Was that the truth?”

“You asked me to tell you.”

The woman behind the counter covered a smile with the back of her hand.

“Vale.”

“Two blocks away. You moved the meeting to nine yesterday.”

“And now I’ve moved it back. People are allowed to change their minds. Important people are allowed to do it before breakfast.”

Adrian glanced at the clock above the ovens.

“Eight minutes.”

“Six.”

The call ended.

He slipped the phone into his coat pocket.

“Trouble?” the woman asked.

“Employment.”

She passed him a paper bag and his coffee. Her eyes lingered on his face, then dropped to the name she had written on the cup.

“You could work somewhere else.”

“I like the health insurance.”

“You never look sick.”

“I’m getting very poor value.”

She laughed. Adrian thanked her and stepped into the morning.

National City was already warm beneath its yellow sun. Delivery trucks occupied lanes they had no intention of surrendering. Someone leaned on a horn. At the corner, a man in running clothes argued with a parking meter.

Adrian waited for the crossing signal.

Beneath his left cuff, a narrow band rested against his wrist. Dark metal, an unobtrusive face, a clasp fitted precisely to his skin. Most people assumed it was an expensive watch.

He had built everything beneath that assumption.

The pressure against his wrist was familiar. His heartbeat remained steady. He heard the traffic nearest him, the conversation beside him, the impatient clicking of a pedestrian’s keys.

The rest of the city lay beyond his senses.

Across the street, the signal changed.

He crossed with everyone else.

***

Kara Danvers was attempting to carry a coffee, a bag, a stack of folders and an increasingly desperate conversation when Adrian reached the executive floor.

“Yes, I understand she said this morning, but she also said—”

The folders began to slide.

Adrian caught the bottom one before it reached the floor. Kara trapped the others against her chest.

“—that she wanted the revised version,” she finished into her headset. “The revised revised version. Thank you.”

She ended the call and exhaled.

“Please tell me that’s yours.”

Adrian looked at the folder in his hand.

“Unless I’ve recently become responsible for a perfume campaign, probably not.”

“Then I need it.”

He gave it back.

Her glasses had slipped down her nose. She pushed them up with the wrist of the hand holding her coffee, considered the risks of that arrangement, and lowered the cup.

Adrian took two of the folders.

“I can carry them.”

“I know. Where are they going?”

“Conference room.”

“So am I.”

They walked together.

Kara’s gaze dropped to the bakery bag.

“Is that—”

“Almond.”

“Oh.”

He looked at her.

“When did you last eat?”

“This morning.”

“What?”

“Something.”

“That sounds filling.”

“It had nutritional value.”

“Coffee isn’t a food group.”

“It should be.”

He offered her the bag. She hesitated for approximately half a second before accepting it.

“Thank you.”

“Try not to get sugar on the revised revised version.”

From the far side of the floor, Winn Schott watched this exchange with the expression of a man witnessing an avoidable diplomatic setback.

Adrian placed the folders in the conference room. By the time he came back, Winn had intercepted Kara at her desk.

“You know,” Winn was saying, “some of us also know where bakeries are.”

Kara, halfway through the pastry, looked up.

“Great. You should go.”

Adrian kept walking.

***

Cat’s office had a view of the city, flowers that would be replaced before they could wilt, and a silence that rarely survived her arrival.

She was standing beside her desk with a tablet in one hand.

“The advertising people have sent me another explanation.”

“That was quick.”

“Explanations are what people manufacture when they’ve failed to manufacture revenue.”

Adrian set his coffee down and opened the folder he had brought upstairs.

“The revised figures are on the first page. The discrepancy is on the second.”

Cat studied him over the tablet.

“Discrepancy.”

“They billed for placement they didn’t deliver.”

“That sounds remarkably like a less polite word.”

“I left room for you to choose it.”

She took the folder.

Through the glass, Adrian could see Kara settling behind her desk. Winn had followed her. She was smiling at something he said while opening three separate windows on her computer.

Cat turned a page.

For several seconds, she read without speaking.

Then she picked up her phone.

“Move my call with their president forward. And find out whether he knows his people sent me these numbers.”

She ended the call before an answer could complicate it.

“You’ll sit in.”

“Of course.”

“And don’t wear that expression.”

“Which one?”

“The one that makes people think you’re about to forgive them.”

Adrian’s mouth moved slightly.

“I’ll make an effort.”

Cat looked back at the figures.

A moment later, without lifting her eyes, she said, “Kara has a job, Adrian.”

“She was carrying four things with two hands.”

“She’s had those hands for years. She should know how they work.”

“She does.”

Now Cat looked up.

Adrian met her gaze without challenging it.

The corner of her mouth tightened.

“Close the door on your way out.”

He did.

***

By late morning, the floor had acquired a new subject of conversation.

James Olsen.

Adrian first saw him near the photography offices, tall and relaxed, speaking to a member of staff who seemed delighted to have found a reason to introduce herself.

A few minutes later, he saw Kara discover who James was.

There was a photograph involved.

Adrian knew that photograph. Most people did. Superman above the world, caught in an instant that made an impossible thing feel strangely reassuring.

Kara’s attention moved from the image to the man beside it.

Her entire face changed.

She had been tired ten minutes earlier. Now she looked as though someone had unexpectedly opened a window.

Adrian paused at the printer long enough to hear the introduction, then collected his pages.

Winn appeared beside him.

“So.”

Adrian aligned the papers against the tray.

“So?”

“That’s James Olsen.”

“Apparently.”

“The James Olsen.”

“Would you like me to ask whether there’s another one?”

Winn ignored that.

“Pulitzer. Superman. Those shoulders.”

Adrian glanced at him.

“The shoulders weren’t listed in the announcement.”

“They didn’t need to be.”

Inside the office, Kara laughed.

Winn’s expression fell a little.

Adrian slid the last page into his folder.

“If you want to ask her to dinner, ask her.”

“I have a strategy.”

“How long have you had it?”

“That is not relevant.”

“Then it must be an excellent strategy.”

Winn pointed at him.

“You’re enjoying this.”

“A little.”

***

The meeting ran late.

Cat extracted an apology, a revised agreement and a concession Adrian had not expected her to get. When it was over, she dismissed the departing executives with a smile that promised an unpleasant future if she saw the same figures twice.

Then she leaned back in her chair.

“Now you may forgive them.”

“I’ll put it on the calendar.”

“Next quarter.”

Adrian gathered the papers.

His cuff shifted.

For a moment, the dark band showed at his wrist.

Cat’s eyes flicked toward it.

“New?”

“No.”

“I’ve never seen it.”

“You usually look at my face when you’re criticizing me.”

“It gives me more material.”

He adjusted his sleeve and left.

In the corridor, a junior employee hurried past with an armful of framed prints. The top frame struck a door handle.

Glass broke.

Adrian stopped.

The sound was small. A clean crack, followed by fragments striking the floor.

For an instant, the corridor was gone.

There had been glass in the windows of his home.

It had caught the red sunlight in the afternoon. Someone had once complained about the colour it cast across the table. He could remember the complaint more clearly than he could remember the sound of the accident.

The accident belonged to a confused sequence of messages, dust and voices giving him answers that would not become different no matter how many times he asked.

What followed was clearer.

That was the difficulty.

He remembered choosing a direction.

He remembered anger becoming action faster than thought could follow.

He remembered faces.

“Mr. Vale?”

The corridor returned.

The employee crouched among the pieces, one hand held awkwardly away from the floor.

A thin red line crossed her finger.

Adrian knelt.

“Leave the glass.”

“I’m sorry. I didn’t see the—”

“It’s a frame.”

“It was for Ms. Grant’s—”

“I’ll tell her.”

He took a clean handkerchief from his pocket and passed it to her.

“Hold that against the cut.”

She obeyed.

Around them, the office continued. A telephone rang. Someone complained about a missing file. A printer started up.

Adrian called maintenance, then went downstairs to buy a replacement frame.

***

By the time Kara reached home, the excitement of meeting James Olsen had become tangled with the familiar frustration of another day spent doing things she was good at and wondering whether they were enough.

Alex listened while Kara searched through clothes.

She had heard versions of this before.

That did not make tonight’s version easier.

“I’m useful,” Kara said, holding a dress against herself. “I know I’m useful.”

Alex sat on the edge of the bed.

“Nobody said you weren’t.”

“I can find a restaurant that will make something that isn’t on the menu. I can get someone on the phone when they’ve spent three days avoiding her. I can remember which person isn’t supposed to sit next to which other person.”

“Those are real skills.”

Kara lowered the dress.

“I know.”

Alex watched her.

The complaint was larger than Cat Grant. They both knew it.

For years, their family had helped make a life in which Kara could belong. School, friends, work, ordinary disappointments. Things that could be survived without anyone learning what she was.

To Alex, those years had been protection.

To Kara, on evenings like this one, they could feel like standing outside a locked door with the key in her hand.

Alex rose and selected the blue outfit.

“This one.”

Kara looked at it.

“You didn’t answer me.”

“I don’t think you asked me a question.”

“That’s very convenient.”

Alex softened.

“Go out. Meet someone. Have one evening that isn’t about work.”

Kara took the clothes.

“And you?”

“A conference. Geneva.”

“Your evening sounds worse.”

“It usually is.”

They smiled at each other.

For a moment, the difficult conversation could wait.

***

Adrian bought tomatoes on his way home.

The grocer insisted the smaller ones were better. Adrian disagreed, tasted a slice offered on the point of a little knife, and admitted he had been wrong.

The grocer accepted this with the dignity of a man whose expertise had finally been acknowledged.

At his apartment building, Adrian held the door for a neighbour struggling with a wheeled shopping bag.

“You’re late,” she told him.

“You’re keeping track?”

“Somebody should.”

She lived on the floor below his and had decided, without consultation, that he needed feeding more often than he fed himself.

He lifted the front of her bag over the threshold.

“Did you eat?”

“I’m about to.”

“Something cooked?”

“That is the plan.”

She examined the grocery bag.

“Good. Too handsome to live on sandwiches.”

Adrian laughed.

Upstairs, he changed out of his work clothes, washed the tomatoes and put water on the stove.

His apartment was comfortable without being impressive. Books occupied more space than ornaments. A half-repaired lamp sat on the end of the dining table beside a small box of tools.

There was no television in the kitchen.

He opened a window, let the evening air in and began cutting vegetables.

A decision about salt occupied him for almost a minute.

He liked that.

***

Kara’s date was going badly.

By the time he found a reason to leave, she had stopped trying to persuade herself otherwise.

She sat for a moment with her drink and the uncomfortable feeling that she had dressed for an evening that had never really begun.

Then the television above the bar caught her attention.

An aircraft.

A flight number.

**237.**

She straightened.

The noise around her receded beneath the broadcast.

Engine failure. Falling altitude. Emergency crews moving into position.

Alex.

Kara was out of her chair before the report finished.

Outside, the city carried on around her. People crossed the pavement. Cars moved through pools of light. Somewhere above all of it, her sister was inside a machine that could no longer keep itself in the air.

She found an alley.

Looked upward.

For a heartbeat, habit held her in place.

Years of lowering her voice. Measuring her strength. Letting other people reach the top shelf. Smiling when she wanted to say that she could do more.

Then she thought of Alex.

Kara pushed off.

Her first attempt ended against brick.

She dropped, caught herself, and looked up again.

There was no time to be embarrassed.

She tried.

This time, the ground fell away.

***

Inside Flight 237, Alex braced as the aircraft shook.

People were praying. Crying. Calling names.

Training reduced the cabin to details she could use and facts she could not change. Exits. Fire. The angle of descent.

Then something moved outside the window.

Alex turned her head.

For one impossible instant, she saw her sister beyond the glass.

Kara.

Fear struck differently then.

Below the aircraft, Kara reached upward.

The underside met her hands with a force that drove a cry from her throat. Metal shuddered. The weight wanted to carry her down with it.

She pushed.

The plane kept descending.

She pushed harder.

Air tore at her clothes. The city rushed toward them. Lights spread beneath the wings, too close, too many.

Ahead, the bridge rose across their path.

Kara adjusted, trying to turn the aircraft while holding it level, discovering with every second that strength and knowing how to use it were different things.

Steel flashed past.

The aircraft struck part of the bridge.

She held on.

Beyond it lay water.

Open space.

A place to put them.

Kara drove upward against the falling weight until the descent began to change.

The surface rushed toward her.

Then the aircraft hit.

Water erupted around the fuselage.

For a moment there was nothing but noise, spray and the enormous violence of a thing stopping that should never have been moving that fast.

Then it stopped.

It stayed afloat.

Inside, frightened voices began to change.

Kara remained beneath it long enough to be certain.

Only then did she let go.

***

Adrian was washing a saucepan when his phone rang.

He dried one hand.

“Vale.”

“Turn on a television.”

Cat.

“What happened?”

“If I have to explain it before you’ve seen it, I’ll have to explain it twice.”

He went into the living room and switched on the screen.

An aircraft rested on the water.

Emergency boats surrounded it.

The picture changed to footage filmed from somewhere onshore: a blurred figure beneath the fuselage, moving against the falling mass.

Adrian became still.

“A woman,” Cat said. “Flying.”

“I can see that.”

“Then appreciate how much less interesting tomorrow morning became for every other media company.”

The footage replayed.

The figure was too distant for a face. He could make out darkened clothes, loose hair, arms raised beneath the aircraft.

The angle was poor. The effort was unmistakable.

Adrian watched her struggle to correct the descent.

Watched the collision.

Watched her hold on.

“Adrian.”

“I’m here.”

“I want transport, staffing and broadcast availability ready before the morning meeting. Find out who has usable footage. Legal handles acquisition. Nobody promises money on my behalf without approval.”

He reached for a pen.

“Understood.”

“And get some sleep. Tomorrow will be unpleasant.”

She hung up.

Adrian wrote down the arrangements, made two calls and sent the necessary messages.

On the screen, passengers were being brought to safety.

Some were crying.

One man stood wrapped in a blanket with both hands over his face.

Adrian put the phone down.

His left sleeve had ridden back while he worked. The band lay against his wrist, quiet and familiar.

The reporter began another account of the rescue.

He listened.

***

Across the city, Kara burst into her apartment, wet, breathless and unable to stop smiling.

She shut the door and pressed her back against it.

For several seconds, she simply stood there.

Then she laughed.

The sound filled the room.

She had done it.

The aircraft. The people.

Alex.

For once, the thing she could do and the thing she had done were the same.

A knock came at the door.

Kara hurried to open it.

Her sister stood on the other side.

Alive.

Kara reached for her.
